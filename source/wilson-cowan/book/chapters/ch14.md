Mean field theory replaces a population by its average. The review lists four reasons to go further: correlations are everywhere in cortical recordings; real populations are finite, so averages fluctuate; mean field theory can get even the *mean* dynamics qualitatively wrong (Chapter 13 showed it never relaxes); and it offers no way to check its own accuracy. This chapter makes the finite-size effects visible in the simplest stochastic population, whose mean field theory is exactly a Wilson–Cowan equation, and introduces the tools for describing fluctuations: the master equation, the moment hierarchy, the Fokker–Planck equation and the linear noise approximation.

::deck

## A population of $N$ two-state neurons

Following Buice and Cowan's stochastic formulation, let each of $N$ neurons be either **active** or **quiescent**. An active neuron becomes quiescent at rate 1; a quiescent neuron becomes active at rate $f(w a + I)$, where $a = n/N$ is the fraction currently active. The state is just the number $n$ of active neurons, and it jumps up or down by one:
$$
n \to n + 1 \ \text{ at rate } (N - n)\,f(wn/N + I), \qquad n \to n - 1 \ \text{ at rate } n. \tag{14.1}
$$
This defines a **master equation** for the probability $P(n, t)$. We can simulate it exactly with the Gillespie algorithm: wait an exponentially distributed time, then make one of the two jumps with probability proportional to its rate.

**Mean field theory.** For large $N$, the change in $a$ per unit time is (rate up − rate down)/$N$:
$$
\dot a = F(a) = -a + (1 - a)\,f(w a + I). \tag{14.2}
$$
This is exactly the one-population Wilson–Cowan equation with refractory factor $(1 - a)$ (Chapter 2): quiescent neurons play the role of “sensitive” ones.

## Fluctuations shrink like $1/\sqrt N$

Simulate (14.1) for different $N$ (Figure 14.1a). Every population fluctuates around the mean field steady state $a^*$; the fluctuations are large for 25 neurons and small for 800. Why $1/N$ for the variance? Each neuron flips independently given $a$, so the number of flips in a short time is roughly Poisson, with variance equal to its mean. The variance of $n$ grows like $N$, so the variance of $a = n/N$ falls like $N/N^2 = 1/N$.

## The linear noise approximation

To quantify this, expand the master equation for large $N$ (van Kampen's system-size expansion). To first order it becomes a **Fokker–Planck equation**, equivalent to a Langevin equation with small noise:
$$
\dot a = F(a) + \sqrt{\frac{G(a)}{N}}\;\xi(t), \qquad G(a) = a + (1 - a)f(wa + I), \tag{14.3}
$$
where $G$ is the *sum* of the up and down rates per neuron (both kinds of jumps add variance) and $\xi$ is white noise. Linearise about $a^*$: a small deviation $x = a - a^*$ obeys $\dot x = F'(a^*)\,x + \sqrt{G(a^*)/N}\,\xi$, an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. A restoring force $F'(a^*) < 0$ balanced against noise of strength $G/N$ gives a Gaussian of variance
$$
\sigma^2 = \frac{G(a^*)}{2\,|F'(a^*)|\,N}. \tag{14.4}
$$
Geometrically, the steeper the flow towards $a^*$, the tighter the cloud.

(b) The distribution of $a$ for $N = 200$ against the Gaussian of the linear noise approximation (14.4). It is close, but the simulated distribution is skewed, a nonlinear effect that the linear noise approximation leaves out. (c) The variance against $N$ approaches (14.4), falling like $1/N$.

## Moments: the hierarchy again

Equations for moments follow from (14.3) by averaging. Expanding $F$ about the mean $\bar a$ and keeping the variance $C$,
$$
\dot{\bar a} = F(\bar a) + \tfrac12F''(\bar a)\,C + \dots, \qquad \dot C = 2F'(\bar a)\,C + \frac{G(\bar a)}{N} + \dots \tag{14.5}
$$
The mean depends on the variance through the curvature $F''$; the variance depends on the third moment, and so on: the **moment hierarchy** of Chapter 13, now for a stochastic system. Mean field theory sets $C = 0$. Keeping $C$ and truncating at second order gives the **generalised activity equations** of Chapter 15.

:::key Why fluctuations shift the mean
If $F$ curves, symmetric noise around $a^*$ does not average to zero drift: the flow at $a^* + x$ and at $a^* - x$ differ by $F''x^2$. A curved flow turns fluctuations into a systematic push, so the true mean differs from the mean field fixed point by an amount proportional to the variance, $\propto 1/N$. Figure 14.1b's skew is the same effect.
:::

## Finite size can change the qualitative behaviour

With strong recurrent excitation the mean field equation (14.2) is bistable (Chapter 2): a low state, a saddle and a high state. Mean field theory says a population started in the low state stays there **forever**. A finite population does not: fluctuations eventually carry it over the barrier (Figure 14.2b).

For large $N$ the stationary distribution of (14.3) has the form $p(a) \propto e^{-N\Phi(a)}$, with the **quasi-potential**
$$
\Phi(a) = -2\int^a \frac{F(x)}{G(x)}\,\dd x, \tag{14.6}
$$
a landscape whose valleys are the stable states and whose ridge is the saddle (Figure 14.2a). Escaping requires a fluctuation of probability $\sim e^{-N\Delta\Phi}$, so the mean switching time grows **exponentially** with $N$ (Kramers' law). For a small cortical population the switches happen within seconds; for a large one they effectively never do. This is how mean field theory can be qualitatively wrong for a finite network: its stable states are only metastable.

(b) Langevin simulations (14.3) in XPPAUT: $N = 40$ hops often between the two states; $N = 80$ rarely. (c) Mean waiting time to switch on from the low state, from exact (Gillespie) simulations: it grows exponentially with $N$, with a fitted slope ($0.063$) close to the barrier height $\Delta\Phi = 0.059$ predicted by (14.6).

:::paper In the review
“Although mean field theory is highly successful in describing neural activity … there are reasons for trying to go beyond it. First, correlations have been found to be prevalent in cortical activity … Second, the number of neurons in any neural population is finite, which can make any mean field approximation susceptible to finite-size effects … Third, mean field theory may not qualitatively describe the mean dynamics correctly … Finally, mean field theory cannot provide a systematic method for evaluating its validity and accuracy.” The review's example of the third point is the Kuramoto model, whose mean field steady state is only marginally stable but which relaxes quickly in simulations because of finite-size effects.
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- A population of $N$ stochastic two-state neurons has an exact **master equation**; its mean field limit is a Wilson–Cowan equation.
- Fluctuations of the population activity have variance $\propto 1/N$; the **linear noise approximation** gives $\sigma^2 = G/(2|F'|N)$.
- Moments obey a **hierarchy**: the mean depends on the variance through the curvature of the flow, so fluctuations shift the mean by $O(1/N)$.
- In a bistable population, finite size turns stable states into metastable ones: switching times grow like $e^{N\Delta\Phi}$.
:::
