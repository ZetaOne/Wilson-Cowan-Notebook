A square millimetre of cortex holds about a hundred thousand neurons. Each one fires brief electrical spikes, each spike nudges thousands of other neurons, and the whole tangle changes from millisecond to millisecond. If we wanted to follow every spike we would need hundreds of thousands of coupled equations, and we would drown in detail before learning anything. The Wilson–Cowan equations are the most famous answer to a simple question: **can we describe a whole population of neurons by one number, its activity, and still understand what it does?** This chapter explains the idea of a population activity, why it can work, and what it throws away. It sets the scene for the rest of the book.

::deck

## The picture first: a raster and a rate

Figure 1.1 shows a network of 200 model neurons, simulated with XPPAUT. Each neuron is a **θ-neuron**, the simplest model of a neuron that fires repetitively when its input is large enough (we meet it properly in Chapter 13). Its state is an angle $\theta$. When $\theta$ passes $\pi$ the neuron fires a spike, and the quantity $1 - \cos\theta$ looks like a voltage trace: flat at rest, a sharp pulse at each spike (panel a). The neurons differ in how excitable they are, and they excite each other weakly through a shared synaptic signal.

::fig ch01_raster | A network of 200 θ-neurons (XPPAUT via pyauto-xpp). (a) Three individual neurons, each spike a pulse of $1-\cos\theta$. (b) The **raster**: one row per neuron, one tick per spike. A stimulus raises every neuron's input between $t = 30$ and $t = 70$ (shaded). (c) The **population rate**: spikes from all neurons counted in short time bins and divided by the number of neurons and the bin width. With very short bins (grey) the count is noisy; with slightly longer bins (magenta) it is a smooth curve $a(t)$.

Look at the three panels from top to bottom, because that is exactly the journey of this book:

- **Single neurons** (panel a) are messy. Each fires at its own times, and nothing about one neuron tells you much about the network.
- **The raster** (panel b) shows a pattern the eye picks up at once: sparse firing, then dense firing while the stimulus is on, then sparse again. That pattern belongs to the *population*, not to any single row.
- **The population rate** (panel c) captures the pattern in a single curve. It rises, overshoots slightly, settles, and falls when the stimulus ends.

The Wilson–Cowan programme is to write a differential equation *for the curve in panel c directly*, without simulating the rows of panel b.

::fig ch01_coarse | Coarse-graining in action. A short window (magenta) slides across the raster; the number of spikes inside it, divided by the number of neurons and the window width, is the population rate plotted below. The animation builds the curve one window at a time.

:::def Population activity
For a population of $N$ neurons, the **population activity** (or population rate) at time $t$ is
$$
a(t) = \frac{1}{N}\,\frac{\text{number of spikes in } [t, t+\Delta t)}{\Delta t},
$$
the average number of spikes per neuron per unit time, measured in a short window $\Delta t$. Wilson and Cowan used a closely related quantity, the **fraction of cells active** at time $t$. Both are numbers between 0 and a maximum set by how fast a neuron can fire.
:::

## Why one number can be enough: coarse-graining

Physics has done this before. The air in a room contains about $10^{27}$ molecules, each moving and colliding chaotically. Yet if we only want to know whether the room is comfortable, three numbers suffice: temperature, pressure and humidity. They are **coarse-grained** variables: averages over many molecules, in which the microscopic detail has cancelled out.

:::idea Coarse-graining and mean field theory (the review's opening)
“Coarse-graining considers the system at a lower resolution, thereby reducing the degrees of freedom of the system. In the process, some information will be lost. The strategy is to ensure that the lost information is not crucial.” **Mean field theory** is the particular coarse-graining in which each unit is assumed to feel only the *average* effect of all the others, and fluctuations around that average are neglected. It was invented for magnets, where each atomic spin feels the average magnetisation of its neighbours. If there are many units and they are not too correlated, the central limit theorem makes the fluctuations small, and the average is all that matters.
:::

Why should the average be enough for neurons? Geometrically, think of each neuron's input as a sum of many small kicks from many other neurons. If the kicks are numerous and roughly independent, their sum is close to its average, with a spread that shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$. So each neuron effectively feels a smooth input determined by the population activity. That smooth input in turn determines how fast each neuron fires, and hence the activity. **The activity drives itself**: that self-consistency is the mean field idea, and Chapters 2–4 turn it into an equation.

## How neurons turn input into rate

To close the loop we need to know how a neuron's firing rate depends on its input: the **f–I curve** (frequency–current curve). For the θ-neuron there is a formula. With constant input $I > 0$ it fires periodically at rate $\sqrt{I}/\pi$; with $I \le 0$ it does not fire at all (Figure 1.3, teal). That is a sharp threshold at $I = 0$.

Real populations are **heterogeneous**: thresholds differ from cell to cell. If we average the f–I curves of many neurons whose thresholds are spread out, the sharp corner is smoothed into a gentle S-shaped rise (Figure 1.3, magenta). Some neurons are already firing when the average input is still negative, and others have not started when it is positive. This smooth curve is the population's **gain function**, and Chapter 3 shows that this is exactly how Wilson and Cowan obtained their famous sigmoid.

::fig ch01_fi | Single-neuron and population gain. Teal: the firing rate of one θ-neuron against its input, measured with XPPAUT (dots) and the exact formula $\sqrt{I}/\pi$ (line); it has a sharp threshold. Magenta: the average rate of 4000 neurons whose thresholds are spread with standard deviation 0.25. The corner is smoothed out: heterogeneity turns a sharp threshold into a smooth population gain.

## The three ingredients of an activity equation

The review lists three ingredients that every activity equation shares:

1. **A continuous, time-dependent activity variable** $a(t)$, standing for a whole population.
2. **A linear weighted sum** of activities in the input: population $i$ receives $\sum_j w_{ij} a_j + I_i$, where $w_{ij}$ is the strength of the connection from population $j$ and $I_i$ is external input.
3. **A nonlinear activation (gain) function** $f$ turning input into activity.

Put together with a relaxation time $\tau$, they give the generic form
$$
\tau\,\dot a_i = -a_i + f\Big(\sum_j w_{ij}\,a_j + I_i\Big). \tag{1.1}
$$
Read it geometrically, one population at a time. The right-hand side says: **activity relaxes towards whatever the gain function says it should be, given the current input.** If $a$ is below $f(\text{input})$ it rises; if above, it falls; and $\tau$ sets how fast. Everything in Parts I and II is a study of what this simple rule does when populations excite and inhibit each other.

:::paper What the review calls the Wilson–Cowan equations
Chow and Karimipanah write the 1972 equations as
$$
\tau\dot a_i = -a_i + (1 - r a_i)\,f_i\Big(\sum_j w_{ij}a_j + I_i(t)\Big),
$$
which is (1.1) with an extra factor $(1 - r a_i)$ for **refractoriness**: a neuron that has just fired cannot fire again at once (Chapter 3). With $r = 0$ it is exactly (1.1). They reserve the name “Wilson–Cowan equations” for this form and call the closely related form for the *inputs*, $\tau\dot s_i = -s_i + \sum_j w_{ij} f_j(s_j) + I_i$, the “STM model” (Chapter 4 shows the two are the same when the input is constant).
:::

## What mean field theory throws away

Coarse-graining always loses something. Two losses will haunt us later.

- **Timing.** In Figure 1.1(c) the rate overshoots just after the stimulus starts, because many neurons that were sitting at rest fire their first spike *together*. A smooth activity equation like (1.1) relaxes monotonically and misses that synchrony. Chapter 12 shows when rate equations fail for exactly this reason.
- **Fluctuations.** With $N = 200$ the rate in panel (c) jitters around its mean. Mean field theory sets those fluctuations to zero. That is fine for $N\to\infty$ but not for finite networks, and Part IV shows how to put them back.

:::recap What to carry forward
- The **population activity** $a(t)$ is the spike rate of a whole population, averaged over a short window.
- **Coarse-graining** keeps averages and discards detail. **Mean field theory** assumes each neuron feels only the average of the others, so fluctuations vanish for large $N$.
- Heterogeneity turns sharp single-neuron thresholds into a smooth, S-shaped **population gain function**.
- Activity equations combine an activity variable, a weighted sum of inputs and a gain function: $\tau\dot a = -a + f(\sum w a + I)$. Activity relaxes towards the gain function's value.
- Mean field theory loses synchrony (fast transients) and fluctuations (finite $N$), the subjects of Chapter 12 and Part IV.
:::

:::try Exercises
1. In Figure 1.1(c), why is the rate with bins of 0.25 noisier than with bins of 1? *(Fewer spikes per bin. The count is roughly Poisson, so its relative error is $1/\sqrt{\text{count}}$.)*
2. A θ-neuron with $I = 0.25$ fires at what rate? *(About $\sqrt{0.25}/\pi \approx 0.16$ spikes per unit time.)*
3. If every neuron in a population had *exactly* the same threshold and input, what would the population gain function look like? *(The single-neuron curve itself, with a sharp corner. Heterogeneity is what smooths it.)*
