Cortex is a sheet. A population at one place excites its neighbours and, through local interneurons, inhibits cells a little further away. In 1973 Wilson and Cowan replaced their two numbers $E(t)$ and $I(t)$ by two **fields** $E(x, t)$ and $I(x, t)$ over position $x$; in 1977 Amari simplified the result to one equation that is still the starting point of neural field theory. This chapter shows what fields can do that point populations cannot: hold a localised **bump** of activity (a memory of *where*), and carry **fronts** and **pulses** that travel.

::deck

## From a sum over populations to an integral over space

In the many-population form of Chapter 4, population $i$ receives $\sum_j w_{ij}\,a_j$. Put the populations on a line, at positions $x$ and $y$, and let the connection strength depend only on the distance between them, $w_{ij} \to w(x - y)$. The sum becomes an integral:
$$
\tau\,\partial_t u(x, t) = -u(x, t) + \int w(x - y)\, f\big(u(y, t)\big)\,\dd y + I(x, t). \tag{8.1}
$$
This is **Amari's equation**, written for the input $u$ (the STM form of Chapter 4); $f(u)$ is the firing rate at position $y$. Wilson and Cowan's 1973 version keeps $E$ and $I$ separate with their own spatial kernels; Amari folds them into one kernel $w$ that can be positive near and negative far.

::fig ch08_kernel | (a) A “Mexican hat” kernel: excitation between nearby cells (pink), inhibition between distant ones (blue). (b) Amari's construction of a bump: the input at the edge of an active region of width $a$ is $W(a) = \int_0^a w(x)\,\dd x$. A bump exists where this equals the threshold $\theta$: one narrow (unstable) and one wide (stable) solution.

## Bumps: a memory of position

**Picture first.** Suppose a region of width $a$ is active and everything else is silent. Each active cell receives excitation from its active neighbours. At the *edge* of the region, the input is the kernel integrated over the active region, $\int_0^a w(x)\,\dd x = W(a)$. For the region to keep its size, the edge must sit exactly at threshold. That is **Amari's condition**
$$
W(a) = \theta \qquad \text{(with } f \text{ a step at } \theta\text{ and no external input).} \tag{8.2}
$$
Geometrically (Figure 8.1b) we intersect the curve $W(a)$ with the horizontal line at height $\theta$.

**Which bump is stable?** Imagine the bump grows slightly. Its edge then receives input $W(a + \delta)$. If $W$ is *decreasing* there, the edge input drops below threshold and the edge retreats: the bump shrinks back. So the wide bump, on the falling side of $W$, is stable; the narrow one, on the rising side, is unstable and acts as a threshold between “no bump” and “bump”. Since $W'(a) = w(a)$, the condition is simply $w(a) < 0$: **the bump is stable when its width reaches into the inhibitory surround.**

With the kernel of Figure 8.1 and $\theta = 0.3$, the stable width is $a = 1.94$. XPPAUT, simulating (8.1) on 240 grid points with a steep sigmoid, finds $a = 1.88$, within half a grid cell.

(b) A strong one ignites a bump that **persists after the stimulus is gone**. (c) The bump's profile: active (above threshold) in the centre, suppressed below rest on either side by the inhibitory surround.

:::idea Working memory
In tasks where a monkey must remember the location of a briefly flashed target, neurons in prefrontal cortex tuned to that location keep firing through the delay. A bump attractor is the standard model: the position of the bump stores the remembered location, and because the kernel depends only on distance, the bump can sit at *any* position (a continuous family of steady states, a **line attractor**).
:::

## Fronts: a wave of switching on

Now take a purely excitatory kernel, $w(x) = \tfrac12 e^{-|x|}$ (total weight 1), with a threshold $\theta < \tfrac12$. The uniform field has two stable states: all off ($u = 0$) and all on ($u = 1$). Switch on the left end. The active region excites cells just ahead of its edge, pushing them over threshold, which then excite the cells ahead of them: a **front** travels into the resting region, like a line of falling dominoes.

**The speed, derived.** Look for a front moving at speed $c$: $u(x, t) = U(\xi)$ with $\xi = x - ct$, active for $\xi < 0$. Then (8.1) with $\tau = 1$ becomes $-cU' = -U + \int_{-\infty}^{0} w(\xi - y)\,\dd y$. For $\xi > 0$ the integral is $\tfrac12 e^{-\xi}$ (the tail of the kernel reaching ahead of the front). Solving this linear equation for $U$ and imposing $U(0) = \theta$ (the edge is at threshold) gives
$$
\frac{1}{2(1 + c)} = \theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c = \frac{1 - 2\theta}{2\theta}. \tag{8.3}
$$
A low threshold gives a fast front; at $\theta = \tfrac12$ the front stalls, because then the on and off states are equally strong.

## Pulses: adding a slow brake

Real cortex does not stay switched on: neurons adapt, and synapses depress. Add a slow negative feedback $v$ (Pinto and Ermentrout, 2001):
$$
\partial_t u = -u + \int w(x - y)f(u(y))\,\dd y - v, \qquad \partial_t v = \epsilon\,(\beta u - v). \tag{8.4}
$$
With $\epsilon$ small, $v$ builds up slowly behind the front and eventually pulls the activity back below threshold. The front acquires a **back**: a travelling **pulse** (Figure 8.5). This is the population analogue of a nerve impulse, and it is how the spreading waves seen in cortical slices treated with disinhibiting drugs are modelled.

::fig ch08_pulse | Adding adaptation $v$ (8.4) turns the front into a travelling pulse. (a) Space–time plot. (b) A snapshot: activity $u$ (magenta) rises at the front, adaptation $v$ (blue) rises behind it and shuts the activity down.

:::recap What to carry forward
- A neural field replaces the population index by position: $\sum_j w_{ij}a_j \to \int w(x - y)f(u(y))\,\dd y$ (Amari's equation).
- **Bumps**: localised activity sustained by local excitation and surround inhibition; widths solve $W(a) = \theta$; stable when $w(a) < 0$. A memory of position.
- **Fronts**: in a bistable excitatory field, the on state invades the off state at speed $c = (1 - 2\theta)/(2\theta)$ for an exponential kernel.
- **Pulses**: slow adaptation gives the front a back.
:::
