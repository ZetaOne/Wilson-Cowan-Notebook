Every activity equation so far was *postulated* or derived under assumptions about averages. The review's own contribution is to start from a definite network of spiking neurons and ask: what exactly is its mean field theory, and is it a Wilson–Cowan equation? This chapter follows that derivation for the simplest spiking neuron with a phase, the θ-neuron, and shows where the gain function comes from, why mean field theory alone never settles down, and how an exact low-dimensional description captures the volleys that defeated rate models in Chapter 12.

::deck

## Neurons as points running around a circle

A neuron that fires repetitively traces a limit cycle; near the cycle its state is just a **phase** $\theta$ (the isochron book develops this in detail). Spiking is then a point running around a circle at speed $F(\theta, s)$, with a spike each time it passes $\theta = \pi$. For the θ-neuron (the phase form of the quadratic integrate-and-fire neuron),
$$
\dot\theta_i = F(\theta_i, s_i) = 1 - \cos\theta_i + (1 + \cos\theta_i)(\eta_i + s_i), \tag{13.1}
$$
where $\eta_i$ is the neuron's excitability and $s_i$ its synaptic input. The synapses sum spikes:
$$
\tau_s\dot s_i = -s_i + \frac1N\sum_j w_{ij}\sum_{\rm spikes}\delta\big(t - t^s_j\big). \tag{13.2}
$$
With $\eta + s > 0$ the neuron fires periodically. It runs slowly near $\theta = 0$, where $F = 2(\eta + s)$ is smallest, and fast near the spike, where $F = 2$ (Figure 13.1a).

## The density picture and the Klimontovich equation

Instead of tracking where each neuron is, describe the population by the **density** of phases. For one neuron, $\eta_i(\theta, t) = \delta(\theta - \theta_i(t))$, a spike of density at its current phase. Two facts turn this into an equation.

**Spikes are a flux.** A spike occurs when the density passes $\theta = \pi$, so the firing rate of neuron $j$ is the flux through $\pi$: $\delta(t - t^s_j) = F(\pi, s_j)\,\eta_j(\pi, t)$. Equation (13.2) becomes $\tau_s\dot s_i = -s_i + \frac1N\sum_j w_{ij}\,F(\pi, s_j)\,\eta_j(\pi, t)$.

**Neurons are conserved.** Neurons move around the circle but are never created or destroyed. Think of cars on a ring road: the density at a point changes only by the difference between cars arriving and leaving. That is the continuity equation,
$$
\partial_t\eta_i(\theta, t) + \partial_\theta\big[F(\theta, s_i)\,\eta_i(\theta, t)\big] = 0, \tag{13.3}
$$
called the **Klimontovich equation** in the kinetic theory of plasmas. Together with the synaptic equation it is an *exact* rewriting of the network, no simpler than the original.

**Steady state: crowded where slow.** If the density is stationary, (13.3) says the flux $F\eta$ is the same everywhere around the circle: $F(\theta)\,\eta(\theta) = C$. So
$$
\eta(\theta) = \frac{C}{F(\theta)}, \qquad C = \Big(\oint \frac{\dd\theta}{F}\Big)^{-1} = \frac1T, \tag{13.4}
$$
like traffic piling up where cars slow down (Figure 13.1b). The flux $C$ is the rate at which neurons pass $\pi$, the **firing rate**, and it equals one over the period.

(b) 200 neurons spread evenly in time along the cycle (XPPAUT): the histogram of their phases matches the steady density $C/F(\theta)$. (c) The same neurons started *uniformly* in phase: the population rate oscillates forever and never settles to the steady rate. The density equation (13.3) has no dissipation.

## Averaging, the moment hierarchy, and mean field theory

To get something simpler we must average, either over the population or over an **ensemble** of networks started from random initial conditions (the route the review takes). Averaging (13.3) and the synaptic equation gives
$$
\partial_t\langle\eta_i\rangle + \partial_\theta\langle F(\theta, s_i)\,\eta_i\rangle = 0, \qquad \tau_s\langle\dot s_i\rangle = -\langle s_i\rangle + \frac1N\sum_j w_{ij}\,\langle F(\pi, s_j)\,\eta_j(\pi, t)\rangle .
$$
The mean of $\eta$ depends on the average of a *product*, $\langle F\eta\rangle$. An equation for that product involves products of three, and so on: a **moment hierarchy** more complicated than the original network.

**Mean field theory** cuts the hierarchy by assuming every average of a product factorises into a product of averages (all cumulants beyond the mean vanish): $\langle F(\theta, s)\eta\rangle \to F(\theta, \langle s\rangle)\,\langle\eta\rangle$. Then
$$
\partial_t\langle\eta_i\rangle + \partial_\theta\big[F(\theta, \langle s_i\rangle)\langle\eta_i\rangle\big] = 0, \qquad
\tau_s\langle\dot s_i\rangle = -\langle s_i\rangle + \frac1N\sum_j w_{ij}\,F(\pi, \langle s_j\rangle)\,\langle\eta_j(\pi, t)\rangle . \tag{13.5}
$$

**Where the gain function comes from.** The synaptic equation has the form of the STM equation (11.1), with the firing rate $F(\pi)\langle\eta(\pi)\rangle$ in place of $f(s)$. If the density is at its steady state (13.4), that rate is $C = 1/T(s)$, a function of the input alone: **the gain function is the steady firing rate**. For a θ-neuron $T = \pi/\sqrt{\eta + s}$, so
$$
f(s) = \frac{\sqrt{\eta + s}}{\pi} \quad (\eta + s > 0), \qquad 0 \text{ otherwise}. \tag{13.6}
$$
Averaging (13.6) over a Lorentzian spread of excitabilities, centre $\eta_0$ and half-width $\Delta$, smooths the square-root corner into a sigmoid-like curve (Figure 13.2): $f = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt2}\sqrt{\eta_0 + s + \sqrt{(\eta_0 + s)^2 + \Delta^2}}$.

::fig ch13_gain | Gain functions derived from θ-neurons. Identical neurons: the square-root onset $\sqrt s/\pi$ of a neuron at a saddle-node on invariant circle. A Lorentzian spread of excitabilities smooths the corner: the population gain function of Chapter 3, now derived.

:::warn Mean field theory alone does not relax
The review stresses that (13.5) is “dissipation free and thus will not relax to steady state from all initial conditions without a noise source or finite-size effects.” Figure 13.1c shows this: a density that starts away from $C/F$ simply rotates forever. The Wilson–Cowan equations are therefore a description of mean field theory **at steady state**. Heterogeneity, noise, or the finite-size fluctuations of Chapter 14 supply the missing relaxation.
:::

## An exact reduction: the Montbrió–Pazó–Roxin equations

For one special case the density equation can be solved exactly. If the excitabilities $\eta_i$ follow a **Lorentzian** distribution, the density relaxes onto a low-dimensional family of shapes (the Ott–Antonsen manifold) described by two numbers: the population rate $r$ and the mean voltage $v$ of the equivalent QIF neurons. They obey (Montbrió, Pazó and Roxin, 2015)
$$
\dot r = \frac{\Delta}{\pi} + 2rv, \qquad \dot v = v^2 + \eta_0 + I(t) + J r - \pi^2 r^2. \tag{13.7}
$$
This is a two-variable **activity equation** that is *exact* for infinitely many neurons. Unlike a first-order rate model, it has a second variable, $v$, which remembers where the population is in its cycle.

::fig ch13_mpr | The populations of Figure 12.1 again, now with the exact mean field (13.7) (black). (a) $\Delta = 0.01$: it reproduces every volley of the spiking network (the network's peaks look lower only because spikes are counted in bins of width 0.2). (b) $\Delta = 0.2$: the damped overshoot. (c) In the $(v, r)$ plane the response is a spiral into the steady state, wide and slowly decaying for nearly identical neurons, tight for heterogeneous ones.

**Geometry of the volleys.** Linearise (13.7) (with $J = 0$) at its steady state $(r^*, v^*)$, where $v^* = -\Delta/(2\pi r^*)$. The Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}2v^* & 2r^*\\ -2\pi^2 r^* & 2v^*\end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $2v^* \pm 2\pi i r^* = -\Delta/(\pi r^*) \pm 2\pi i r^*$. The steady state is a stable **focus**: it decays at a rate set by $\Delta$ and rotates at the firing frequency $r^*$. A step of input moves the steady state; the old state now lies on a spiral around the new one. For small $\Delta$ the spiral is wide and slow to decay: a train of volleys. A first-order rate model has a single variable, so it cannot spiral at all.

:::paper In the review
The review derives (13.3)–(13.5) for a general phase-model network, writing the gain function as the steady-state flux at $\pi$, and concludes: “Thus the WC equations are a description of mean field theory of the steady state.” It adds that the formalism “could be applied to any type of neuron and synaptic dynamics,” and that “no matter what average is used, we still do not know if or when mean field theory is valid. To answer this question, we need to compute the corrections to mean field theory …” Those corrections are the subject of the last two chapters.
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- A spiking neuron near its cycle is a point running around a circle; a population is a **density** on the circle.
- Conservation of neurons gives the **Klimontovich equation** $\partial_t\eta + \partial_\theta(F\eta) = 0$; spikes are the flux through $\pi$.
- Averaging creates a **moment hierarchy**; mean field theory closes it by factorising all moments.
- At steady state the density is $C/F$ and the flux $C = 1/T(s)$ is the **gain function**: Wilson–Cowan equations are mean field theory at steady state.
- Mean field theory alone has no dissipation. For Lorentzian heterogeneity the exact two-variable MPR equations capture volleys and their decay.
:::
