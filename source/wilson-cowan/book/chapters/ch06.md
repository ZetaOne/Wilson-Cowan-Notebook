Chapter 5 ended with a network that *rings*: kicked, it spirals back to rest. This chapter turns the ringing into a sustained rhythm. Wilson and Cowan showed that with strong enough recurrent excitation and a suitable input, the E–I pair settles onto a **limit cycle**, a closed orbit that attracts its neighbours, and that its frequency rises steadily with the input. Cortical rhythms (alpha, gamma) are commonly modelled this way. We reproduce their Figure 11, find where in input the oscillation lives, and end with its phase description (isochrons and phase response curves).

::deck

## The picture first: why an E–I loop can cycle

Look at the plane before any algebra. In Chapter 5 three steady states sat on the two arms of the hump of the $E$-nullcline. Now change the parameters so that the $I$-nullcline crosses the hump **only once, on its rising middle part** (Figure 6.1a). Follow the flow around that single crossing:

1. **Right of the hump** $E$ grows, because self-excitation wins: the flow points right and up.
2. As $E$ grows, **inhibition builds up** with a lag: the flow turns upward.
3. Above the hump the $E$ flow reverses: **$E$ collapses** under the inhibition. The flow points left.
4. With $E$ low, **$I$ decays**, and the state drifts down until self-excitation can ignite again.

The flow circulates. Whether it circulates *into* the steady state (damped ringing, Chapter 5) or *away* from it decides whether there is a rhythm. On the middle branch of the hump the $E$ equation is self-amplifying (the slope of $\dot E$ in $E$ is positive there), so a steady state on that branch pushes trajectories outward. They cannot escape to infinity, because $E$ and $I$ are bounded between 0 and $k$. Trapped between an outward push near the centre and a bounded box outside, they must settle on a closed orbit. This is the **Poincaré–Bendixson theorem** (Strogatz §7.3) in action.

:::key The geometric recipe for an E–I oscillator
- **One** crossing of the nullclines,
- on the **rising (middle) branch** of the $E$-nullcline's hump, where self-excitation makes the steady state repel,
- with **inhibition following excitation** around the loop.

Strong recurrent excitation ($c_1$) makes the hump; excitation of inhibition ($c_3$) and inhibition of excitation ($c_2$) make the loop.
:::

## Wilson and Cowan's Figure 11

With $c_1 = 16$, $c_2 = 12$, $c_3 = 15$, $c_4 = 3$, $a_E = 1.3$, $\theta_E = 4$, $a_I = 2$, $\theta_I = 3.7$, $r = 1$ and constant input $P = 1.25$, the nullclines cross once, on the middle branch. The steady state there is an **unstable focus** (trace $0.23 > 0$, determinant $3.5 > 0$), and every trajectory winds onto a limit cycle of period $T \approx 5.0\,\tau$ (Figure 6.1).

::fig ch06_fig11 | Wilson and Cowan's Figure 11, recomputed with XPPAUT. (a) Nullclines, streamlines, the single unstable steady state (open circle), a trajectory spiralling out from near it (blue) and the limit cycle (magenta). (b) $E(t)$ and $I(t)$ on the cycle: inhibition peaks after excitation.

Read Figure 6.1b against the loop above: $E$ shoots up, $I$ follows, $E$ collapses, $I$ decays, repeat. The **lag of $I$ behind $E$** is the whole story; without it (inhibition instantaneous) the loop would close at once and there would be no rhythm.

## Where in input does the rhythm live?

Wilson and Cowan reported that oscillations appear only in a window of input $P$ and that the frequency increases with $P$. To see the window, follow the steady states and the cycle as $P$ changes.

**Steady states exactly.** On a steady state, $I$ lies on the $I$-nullcline, $I = I_I(E)$, and the $E$ equation can be solved for $P$. So the steady-state curve can be written as $P(E)$: pick $E$, read off $P$. No root-finding is needed, and folds are simply the turning points of $P(E)$. Stability comes from the trace and determinant of the Jacobian at each point (Strogatz §5.2).

**Cycles with AUTO.** The periodic orbits are followed with XPPAUT's continuation package AUTO, starting at the Hopf point.

::fig ch06_auto | (a) Bifurcation diagram in the input $P$. Black: steady states (solid stable, dashed unstable), from the exact curve $P(E)$. Magenta: the largest and smallest $E$ on the limit cycle, followed by AUTO. Oscillations exist between a homoclinic bifurcation at $P \approx 1.07$ and a Hopf bifurcation at $P \approx 1.88$. (b) Frequency $1/T$ along the cycle: it rises steadily with $P$, from near zero at the homoclinic end.

The diagram has three landmarks, each with a picture in the plane:

- **Hopf bifurcation, $P \approx 1.88$.** For large input the single steady state sits on the right arm of the hump, past the top, and is a stable focus (ringing, as in Chapter 5). Lower $P$ and the crossing slides onto the rising middle branch: the spiral switches from *in* to *out*, the trace of the Jacobian passes through zero with the determinant positive, and a small cycle is born around it (Figure 6.3). The cycle grows smoothly from zero amplitude: a **supercritical Hopf bifurcation** (Strogatz §8.2).
- **Folds, $P \approx 0.91$ and $1.09$.** For small input the curve folds twice: an S-shaped steady-state curve, just like the one-population S-curve of Chapter 2. Between the folds there are three steady states: a low rest state (stable node), a saddle, and the unstable focus.
- **Homoclinic bifurcation, $P \approx 1.07$.** Lowering $P$ from the Hopf point, the cycle grows until it touches the saddle. At that moment the orbit becomes a loop from the saddle back to itself (a **homoclinic orbit**) and the cycle is destroyed. Close to the saddle the flow is very slow, so the period grows without bound and the frequency drops towards zero.

::fig ch06_hopf | Lowering $P$ through the Hopf point. Above $P = 1.88$ the trajectory spirals into the steady state; below, it spirals out onto a cycle that grows as $P$ decreases.

:::idea Frequency is coded by input
Because the period diverges at the homoclinic end and is finite at the Hopf end, the frequency in Figure 6.2b climbs from near zero to about $0.42$ cycles per $\tau$ as $P$ increases. This is Wilson and Cowan's observation that “the frequency of the oscillation is a monotonic function of stimulus intensity”. A population oscillator can therefore turn input strength into rhythm frequency, which is one proposal for how stimulus intensity might be coded in cortical rhythms.
:::

:::warn A thin window of bistability
Between $P \approx 1.07$ (homoclinic) and $P \approx 1.09$ (fold) the stable rest state and the limit cycle coexist. Which one the network shows depends on where it starts: a small instance of the hysteresis of Chapter 7. In the plane, the rest state sits just outside the cycle, separated from it by the stable manifold of the saddle.
:::

## Phase, isochrons and the phase response curve

A stable limit cycle defines a **phase**: a clock that runs once around the orbit per period. The isochron book develops this in detail; here we only show what it looks like for the Wilson–Cowan oscillator.

- Every point attracted to the cycle eventually moves in step with one point on it. Points sharing the same asymptotic phase form a curve, an **isochron**. Figure 6.4a shows eight isochrons, equally spaced in phase. Each passes through the dot of its colour on the cycle.
- Where isochrons crowd together, a small kick changes the phase a lot; where they spread apart, it barely matters. The **phase response curve** (PRC) measures this: the phase shift per unit kick, as a function of the phase at which the kick arrives.

::fig ch06_phase | (a) Isochrons of the Wilson–Cowan oscillator ($P = 1.25$), computed with XPPAUT by integrating from a 161 × 161 grid of starting points and reading off the phase each settles to (background colour). They wind many times around the cycle: the cycle attracts weakly (each turn shrinks a perturbation only to $0.35$ of its size) while the flow spins fast, so a starting point slides a long way round before it locks on. (b) Phase response curves to small kicks in $E$ (magenta) and in $I$ (blue), from XPPAUT's adjoint method. Exciting $E$ advances the rhythm during part of the cycle and delays it elsewhere; exciting $I$ does roughly the opposite.

The winding is itself geometry worth reading. Inside the cycle the isochrons spiral into the unstable focus, every phase crowding in, so near the centre the phase is extremely sensitive to position. Outside, they spread out. The large values of the PRC (up to about 20 phase-units per unit kick) reflect this crowding near the cycle.

Read the PRC from the picture. A kick to $E$ moves the state horizontally to the right. Whether that lands on an isochron of later phase (an advance) or earlier phase (a delay) depends on how the isochrons are tilted where the kick happens. The PRC is exactly the gradient of the phase across the isochrons, evaluated on the cycle.

:::paper In the review
The review records that Wilson and Cowan found “limit cycles, where the frequency of the oscillation is a monotonic function of stimulus intensity”, and that “the combination of mathematical tractability and dynamical richness is the reason for the lasting legacy of their equations.” Phase reduction (isochrons and PRCs), not covered in the review, is the standard tool for coupling such oscillators: two weakly coupled Wilson–Cowan oscillators interact only through their phases.
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- An E–I pair oscillates when the nullclines cross **once, on the rising branch of the hump**: self-excitation makes the steady state repel, delayed inhibition closes the loop, and Poincaré–Bendixson guarantees a cycle.
- Wilson and Cowan's Figure 11: an unstable focus surrounded by a limit cycle of period about $5\tau$.
- In the input $P$ the rhythm lives between a **homoclinic** bifurcation (period → ∞) and a **supercritical Hopf** bifurcation (amplitude → 0). Its frequency rises monotonically with input.
- A limit cycle has a phase; isochrons are curves of equal phase, and the PRC is the phase's sensitivity to kicks.
:::
