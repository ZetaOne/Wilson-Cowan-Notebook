Chapter 2 showed that one excitatory population can be bistable: as its input rises and falls it jumps between a low and a high state at different thresholds, a **hysteresis loop**. Wilson and Cowan showed that the E–I pair does the same, and more: two separate loops, or overlapping loops with **three** stable states at once. This chapter explains all of it with one geometric move, sliding a nullcline, and then draws the map of behaviours over two inputs at once.

::deck

## The one geometric move: input slides the $E$-nullcline

Look at the $E$ equation at a steady state, $E = (k_E - r_E E)\,\mathcal S_E(c_1E - c_2I + P)$. Solve it for $I$:
$$
I = \frac{c_1E + P - \mathcal S_E^{-1}\!\big(E/(k_E - r_EE)\big)}{c_2}.
$$
The input $P$ appears only in the numerator, added to everything else. So **raising $P$ lifts the whole $E$-nullcline straight up by $P/c_2$**, without changing its shape. The $I$-nullcline does not contain $P$ at all and stays put. Everything in this chapter follows from watching the hump slide up and down past the fixed $I$-nullcline (Figure 7.1).

::fig ch07_nullslide | Input $P$ slides the $E$-nullcline (green) vertically; the dashed curve is its position at $P = 0$. (a) $P = -0.6$: the hump is pushed down and crosses the $I$-nullcline only near rest. (b) $P = 0$: three crossings, two stable states and a saddle (Chapter 5). (c) $P = +0.6$: the hump is lifted and only the active crossing survives.

As the hump rises, crossings appear and disappear in pairs. Each pair is born or destroyed where the hump is **tangent** to the $I$-nullcline: a saddle-node (fold) bifurcation, exactly as in Chapter 2, where a sigmoid touched the diagonal.

::fig ch07_slidegif | Animated: the hump sliding up and down as $P$ is ramped. Watch two crossings collide and vanish at each tangency.

## Wilson and Cowan's Figure 5: a hysteresis loop

Plot the steady-state $E$ against $P$ (Figure 7.3a). As in Chapter 6 the curve can be computed exactly: on a steady state $I$ is fixed by the $I$-nullcline, and the equation above then gives $P$ for each $E$. The result is an S-shaped curve with two folds, at $P \approx -0.40$ and $P \approx 0.30$.

::fig ch07_fig5 | Wilson and Cowan's Figure 5, recomputed. (a) Steady states against input $P$ (solid stable, dashed unstable; diamonds are folds). Coloured: a simulation in which $P$ is ramped slowly up from $-0.8$ to $0.8$ (magenta) and back down (blue). The network jumps up near the right fold and down near the left fold. (b) The same ramp against time.

Read the loop as a story. Start at rest with negative input. Raise $P$: the network stays near rest **until the rest state is destroyed** at the right fold, then jumps to the active state. Now lower $P$: it stays active well below the switch-on input, **until the active state is destroyed** at the left fold. Between the folds the state depends on history: the network *remembers* whether it was recently driven hard. Wilson and Cowan proposed this as a substrate for short-term memory.

:::idea Why the jump comes a little late
In Figure 7.3a the upward jump happens slightly past the fold at $P = 0.30$. Near a fold the flow is very slow (the "ghost" of the vanished fixed point, Strogatz §4.3), so a ramped input overshoots before the state escapes. The slower the ramp, the closer the jump lies to the fold.
:::

## Figures 6 and 7: two loops, and three stable states

A hump can cross a rising curve up to five times if both have enough wiggle. Wilson and Cowan found parameter sets where the steady-state curve folds **four** times (Figure 7.4).

::fig ch07_fig67 | (a) Wilson and Cowan's Figure 6: the curve folds four times, giving two separate hysteresis loops, $-0.20 < P < 0.16$ and $0.41 < P < 0.94$. (b) Their Figure 7: the loops overlap, and for $-0.13 < P < 0.21$ (shaded) there are **three** stable steady states. The orange square marks a Hopf point on the middle branch.

In (a) the network has three regimes (low, middle, high activity) and switches between neighbours with hysteresis at each. In (b) the loops overlap so much that low, middle and high states all coexist: a three-level memory. Geometrically, five crossings of the nullclines alternate stable, saddle, stable, saddle, stable along the $I$-nullcline, just as in Chapter 5 three crossings alternated.

## Two inputs at once: the $(P, Q)$ map

So far only $P$ varied. Varying both inputs $P$ (to $E$) and $Q$ (to $I$) gives a map of the plane of inputs, divided by **bifurcation curves** into regions of qualitatively different behaviour (Figure 7.5). Take the oscillator parameters of Chapter 6, and for each $Q$ compute the steady-state curve in $P$. Its folds trace the **fold curves**; the points where the trace of the Jacobian vanishes (with positive determinant) trace the **Hopf curve**.

::fig ch07_twopar | Two-parameter map for the oscillator parameters of Chapter 6 ($\star$: their Figure 11). Blue: fold curves, across which two steady states are born or die. Orange: the Hopf curve, inside which the steady state is unstable and the network oscillates. The two fold curves meet at a cusp; the Hopf curve ends on a fold curve at Bogdanov–Takens points.

Three geometric facts organise the map:

- **Cusp.** The two fold curves meet at a point. Approaching it, the two folds of the S-curve merge and the S straightens out. This is the cusp of Chapter 2 again: below it hysteresis, above it a smooth response.
- **Bogdanov–Takens point.** Where the Hopf curve touches a fold curve, the steady state has a double zero eigenvalue: it is about to vanish (fold) and to change stability (Hopf) at once. Near such a point a homoclinic curve also emanates (not drawn), which is where the cycle of Chapter 6 died at its low-input end.
- **Regions.** Inside the Hopf curve: oscillations. Between the fold curves: three steady states (bistability). Elsewhere: one stable steady state.

:::key Reading a two-parameter map
Each curve is a place where the phase plane changes its *type*: a pair of crossings appears (fold), or a crossing starts to spiral outwards (Hopf). Pick a point on the map, and you can draw the phase portrait from the region's label. Cross a curve, and you know exactly which event happened.
:::

:::paper In the review
“Using dynamical systems analysis, they showed that their equations exhibit multistability and hysteresis, which could serve as a substrate for memory.” The full two-parameter analysis came later, outside the review: Borisyuk and Kirillov (1992) mapped the $(P, Q)$ plane of the Wilson–Cowan pair, finding the cusp and Bogdanov–Takens points drawn above.
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- Input $P$ lifts the $E$-nullcline by $P/c_2$; its crossings with the fixed $I$-nullcline appear and vanish in pairs at **folds**.
- The steady-state curve against $P$ is S-shaped: **hysteresis**, a memory of recent input (Wilson & Cowan Fig. 5).
- With more folds come two loops (Fig. 6) or three coexisting stable states (Fig. 7).
- In the $(P, Q)$ plane fold and Hopf curves divide the inputs into regions of rest, bistability and oscillation, meeting at a **cusp** and at **Bogdanov–Takens** points.
:::
