With two populations the state is a point $(E, I)$ in a plane, and the Wilson–Cowan equations become a vector field on that plane. Everything we know about planar systems (nullclines, fixed points, linearisation, Strogatz chapters 5–6) now applies. This chapter reads the E–I plane the way Wilson and Cowan did in their 1972 paper, reproducing three of their figures: the phase plane with three steady states (Figure 4), the strength–duration curve (Figure 9) and the damped evoked response (Figure 10).

::deck

## Nullclines first

The $E$-**nullcline** is the curve where $\dot E = 0$, so the flow there is purely vertical; the $I$-nullcline is where $\dot I = 0$, so the flow there is purely horizontal. Where they cross, both vanish: fixed points.

**The $I$-nullcline is simple.** Inhibitory cells receive excitation $c_3E$ and inhibit each other. For each $E$, $\dot I = 0$ has exactly one solution $I$, increasing with $E$: more excitation means more inhibition. So the $I$-nullcline is a rising curve (red in the figures).

**The $E$-nullcline has an N shape (a hump).** Freeze $I$ and look at $E$ alone. With $I$ fixed, the $E$ equation is the one-population model of Chapter 2, with recurrent excitation $c_1$ and an input $P - c_2 I$ that *decreases* as $I$ grows. Chapter 2 showed that such a population has one or three steady states depending on its input. So for each frozen $I$ there are one or three zeros of $\dot E$. Stack those zeros for every value of $I$ and they trace a curve that rises and then falls back: the hump (Figure 5.1). It is the S-curve of Chapter 2, rotated and reflected, because here inhibition plays the role of negative input.

::fig ch05_nullgeom | Why the $E$-nullcline has a hump. (a) With $I$ frozen at three values, $\dot E$ as a function of $E$ has three zeros (small $I$) or one (large $I$): the one-population picture of Chapter 2. (b) Plotting the zeros for every $I$ gives the $E$-nullcline. The dashed lines are the three frozen values from (a).

:::key The geometric rule of thumb
The hump of the $E$-nullcline comes from **recurrent excitation** ($c_1$ large). The rising $I$-nullcline comes from **excitation of inhibition** ($c_3$). How they cross decides everything: crossing three times gives multistability (this chapter and Chapter 7); crossing once on the rising side of the hump gives oscillations (Chapter 6).
:::

## Wilson and Cowan's Figure 4: three steady states

With the parameters of their Figure 4 ($c_1 = 12$, $c_2 = 4$, $c_3 = 13$, $c_4 = 11$, $a_E = 1.2$, $\theta_E = 2.8$, $a_I = 1$, $\theta_I = 4$, $r = 1$, $P = Q = 0$) the nullclines cross three times (Figure 5.2).

::fig ch05_fig4 | Wilson and Cowan's Figure 4, recomputed with XPPAUT: the $E$-nullcline (green), the $I$-nullcline (red), streamlines (grey) and four trajectories. Three steady states: rest at the origin (stable), an active state at $(0.440, 0.226)$ (stable), and a saddle between them at $(0.190, 0.068)$ (unstable).

The stability of each fixed point comes from the eigenvalues of the Jacobian there (XPPAUT computes them):

| steady state | $(E, I)$ | eigenvalues | type |
|---|---|---|---|
| rest | $(0, 0)$ | $-0.61,\ -1.13$ | stable node |
| middle | $(0.190, 0.068)$ | $+0.72,\ -1.64$ | saddle |
| active | $(0.440, 0.226)$ | $-1.32,\ -2.75$ | stable node |

The saddle's stable manifold (the curve of states that flow into it) is the **separatrix** between the two basins. Start below it and the network decays to rest; start above it and it switches on. This is the two-population version of the threshold between the low and high states of Chapter 2.

::fig ch05_kicks | Kicks of growing size along $E$ from rest. Small kicks decay back to rest; once a kick crosses the separatrix (the stable manifold of the saddle, open circle), the trajectory runs to the active state.

## How strong, how long? The strength–duration curve

A real stimulus is not an instantaneous kick but a pulse of input $P$ lasting some time. To switch the network on, the pulse must push the state across the separatrix. A short pulse must be strong; a long pulse can be weaker, because it has time to accumulate. Wilson and Cowan computed the threshold intensity as a function of duration (their Figure 9). We recompute it by bisection, running XPPAUT for each trial pulse (Figure 5.4).

::fig ch05_strength | Wilson and Cowan's Figure 9: the smallest pulse intensity that switches the network from rest to the active state, against pulse duration. Short pulses need high intensity; for long pulses the threshold levels off at a minimum (dashed) below which no duration suffices.

Neurophysiologists will recognise this as the population version of the classical **strength–duration curve** of a single axon. The asymptote has a clean geometric meaning: it is the smallest constant input for which the rest state disappears, or at least for which the trajectory can leave it before the pulse ends.

## Damped oscillations: an evoked response

Not every steady state is a node. When the Jacobian has complex eigenvalues $\sigma \pm i\omega$ with $\sigma<0$, the steady state is a **stable focus**: trajectories spiral into it. A brief stimulus then produces a damped oscillation as the network returns to rest. Wilson and Cowan suggested this as a model of the **average evoked potential**, the damped ripple recorded from the scalp after a brief sensory stimulus (their Figure 10).

::fig ch05_evoked | Wilson and Cowan's Figure 10, recomputed. (a) After a brief input pulse (orange), $E - I$ (a proxy for the net current an electrode records) overshoots, undershoots and settles back: a damped oscillation. (b) In the phase plane the same response is a loop spiralling back into the resting state, a stable focus with eigenvalues $-0.20 \pm 0.53i$.

Why does an E–I pair ring? Follow the loop in Figure 5.5(b). The pulse raises $E$. More $E$ excites $I$, which lags behind. When $I$ catches up it suppresses $E$ below rest, then $I$ itself decays, letting $E$ recover. **Delayed negative feedback** produces oscillation, and this is the seed of the sustained rhythms of Chapter 6. With these parameters the rest state's basin is small: a much stronger pulse carries the state into a different steady state instead of back to rest.

:::recap What to carry forward
- The $I$-nullcline rises; the $E$-nullcline has a hump, because self-excitation makes $E$ alone bistable for small $I$.
- Their intersections are the steady states. With three intersections there are two stable states and a saddle, whose stable manifold is the switching threshold (separatrix).
- Switching needs a pulse that crosses the separatrix: short pulses must be strong (strength–duration curve).
- A stable focus responds to brief input with damped oscillations, an evoked response, through delayed inhibitory feedback.
:::
