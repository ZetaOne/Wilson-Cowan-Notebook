Under migraine, flickering light, near-sleep states or certain drugs, people see the same few geometric patterns: tunnels, funnels, spirals, and lattices of blobs or honeycombs. In 1928 Heinrich Klüver called them **form constants**. In 1979 Ermentrout and Cowan explained them with a neural field: when the uniform activity of primary visual cortex becomes unstable, it breaks into **stripes or spots**, and the wiring from the eye to the cortex turns those stripes and spots into exactly the form constants. This chapter follows that argument, which is the classic application of Turing's pattern-forming instability to the brain.

::deck

## The question: when does a uniform state break into a pattern?

Take the field of Chapter 8, $\partial_t u = -u + \int w(x - y)f(u(y))\,\dd y + I$, with a **Mexican hat** kernel (near excitation, far inhibition), and suppose the input $I$ holds it at a uniform steady state $u_0$. Is $u_0$ stable?

**Linearise.** Put $u = u_0 + \delta u$ and keep first-order terms. With $\gamma = f'(u_0)$ the **gain** at the steady state,
$$
\partial_t\,\delta u = -\delta u + \gamma\int w(x - y)\,\delta u(y)\,\dd y. \tag{9.1}
$$

**Try a wave.** Because the kernel depends only on distance, a sinusoid in space stays a sinusoid: convolution with $w$ just multiplies $e^{ikx}$ by the Fourier transform $\hat w(k) = \int w(x)e^{-ikx}\,\dd x$. So with $\delta u = e^{\lambda t}e^{ikx}$, (9.1) gives the **dispersion relation**
$$
\lambda(k) = -1 + \gamma\,\hat w(k). \tag{9.2}
$$
Each wavelength grows or decays on its own, at rate $\lambda(k)$.

**Picture it.** For a Mexican hat, $\hat w(k)$ is *negative* at $k = 0$ (inhibition wins over long distances) and *peaks* at some $k_c > 0$ (Figure 9.1a). Increase the gain $\gamma$: the curve $\lambda(k)$ is stretched upwards, and the first place it crosses zero is its peak, at $k_c$ (Figure 9.1b). At that moment the uniform state becomes unstable to a **pattern of wavelength $2\pi/k_c$**, while uniform perturbations ($k = 0$) still decay. This is a **Turing instability**: it is the shape of the kernel, not any single neuron, that sets the pattern's scale.

::fig ch09_dispersion | (a) The Fourier transform of the Mexican hat of Chapter 8: negative at $k = 0$, peaked at $k_c = 1.09$. (b) The growth rate $\lambda(k)$ for three gains. At the critical gain $\gamma_c = 1/\hat w(k_c)$ the peak touches zero; above it a band of wavenumbers around $k_c$ grows (shaded).

:::key Why near excitation and far inhibition make stripes
Imagine a small bump of activity. Local excitation makes it grow; lateral inhibition suppresses its surroundings; just beyond the suppressed ring, cells are released from inhibition and become active themselves. Each active spot thus seeds a ring of silence and then a ring of activity at a fixed distance: a periodic pattern whose period is set by the reach of excitation and inhibition.
:::

## Watching it happen on a ring

Simulate the field on a ring of 256 populations in XPPAUT, with the uniform state at the steepest point of the sigmoid and gain $1.3\,\gamma_c$, starting from tiny random noise (Figure 9.2). Nothing visible happens at first; then the fastest-growing wavelengths emerge and saturate into a regular pattern.

::fig ch09_ring | XPPAUT simulation of a ring of 256 populations from small noise. (a) Space–time plot: a periodic pattern grows out of the noise and saturates. (b) Final profile. On a ring of length 64 only wavenumbers $2\pi n/64$ fit; the pattern chooses $n = 10$, close to the fastest-growing $k_c$ (the predicted wavelength is 5.76, the ring permits 6.40 or 5.82).

## In two dimensions: stripes or spots

On a sheet, every direction of the wave vector $\vec k$ with $|\vec k| = k_c$ is equally unstable. Which combination wins is decided by the nonlinearity, not by the linear analysis:

- if the gain function is symmetric about $u_0$ (no quadratic term), **stripes** win: one direction of $\vec k$;
- if it is asymmetric (the threshold above or below the resting level), three waves at 120° reinforce each other and **hexagons** win: spots, or holes.

::fig ch09_2d | Patterns on a $40 \times 40$ cortical sheet grown from noise (integrated with FFT convolution in NumPy, since XPP's convolution is one-dimensional). Left: symmetric gain gives stripes. Middle and right: shifting the threshold above or below the uniform level gives spots or holes on a hexagonal lattice.

## From cortex to what is seen: the log-polar map

The final step is pure geometry. The map from the retina to primary visual cortex is, to a good approximation, a **complex logarithm**: a point at distance $r$ and angle $\varphi$ in the visual field lands at cortical position
$$
(x_1, x_2) = (\log r,\ \varphi). \tag{9.3}
$$
The fovea is greatly magnified (small $r$ is stretched by the logarithm), and circles around the centre of gaze become vertical lines in cortex.

So a pattern of activity in cortex is *seen* as its image under the inverse map, $r = e^{x_1}$, $\varphi = x_2$:

- **vertical stripes** (constant $x_1$) are circles: a **tunnel**;
- **horizontal stripes** (constant $x_2$) are rays: a **funnel**;
- **oblique stripes** (constant $x_1 \cos\alpha + x_2 \sin\alpha$) are logarithmic spirals: a **spiral**;
- **hexagonal spots** become a lattice of blobs that grow with distance from the centre: the **honeycomb and cobweb** form constants.

::fig ch09_logpolar | Ermentrout and Cowan's explanation of Klüver's form constants. Top: stripes and spots on the cortical sheet. Bottom: what the observer sees, obtained by mapping each cortical point back to the visual field with $r = e^{x_1}$, $\varphi = x_2$.

:::idea Why the same patterns in everyone
The patterns depend only on two things shared by all human brains: the Mexican-hat shape of cortical connectivity (which fixes the wavelength) and the log-polar retino-cortical map (which fixes the geometry). Drugs or flicker raise the effective gain $\gamma$ past $\gamma_c$; the rest is symmetry. Bressloff, Cowan and colleagues (2001) extended the theory with orientation preference in V1, which adds lattice patterns of oriented edges.
:::

:::paper In the review
“The seminal work of Ermentrout and Cowan (1979), which explained the emergence of visual hallucination patterns in terms of a Turing instability, considered a Wilson–Cowan model in two spatial dimensions. Others (Ben-Yishai et al. 1995; Bressloff et al. 2002) have taken $x$ to be an orientational angle.”
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- Linearise a neural field about a uniform state: each wavenumber grows at rate $\lambda(k) = -1 + \gamma\hat w(k)$.
- A Mexican hat has $\hat w$ peaked at $k_c > 0$: raising the gain destabilises a **pattern** of wavelength $2\pi/k_c$ first (Turing instability).
- In 2-D the nonlinearity selects stripes (symmetric gain) or hexagons (asymmetric gain).
- The retino-cortical map is a complex logarithm; through it, cortical stripes and spots are seen as tunnels, funnels, spirals and lattices: Klüver's form constants.
:::
