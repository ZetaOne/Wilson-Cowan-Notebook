Before two populations can talk to each other, we should understand what one population does on its own when it excites itself. The equation is the simplest activity equation there is,
$$
\tau\,\dot a = -a + f(w a + I), \tag{2.1}
$$
one variable, a recurrent weight $w>0$ and an external input $I$. It is one-dimensional, so its whole behaviour can be read off a single picture. That picture already contains the three ideas Wilson and Cowan used to explain memory in cortex: **multiple stable states, hysteresis and switching**. This chapter is pure geometry, in the style of Strogatz's chapters on flows on the line.

::deck

## The gain function

Throughout this chapter $f$ is the logistic sigmoid
$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \e^{-\beta (x - \theta)}},
$$
which rises smoothly from 0 to 1. It is centred at the **threshold** $\theta$ (here $\theta = 5$), where $f = \tfrac12$, and its steepness is set by the **gain** $\beta$ (here $\beta = 1$). The maximum slope is $\beta/4$, reached at the threshold. Chapter 3 explains where this shape comes from.

## Fixed points are where two curves meet

A fixed point satisfies $\dot a = 0$, that is,
$$
a = f(w a + I).
$$
**Geometrically: draw the diagonal line $y = a$ and the curve $y = f(wa + I)$; the fixed points are where they cross.** That is all. Figure 2.1 does it for three inputs.

::fig ch02_graphical | Fixed points of (2.1) as crossings of the gain curve $f(wa+I)$ (black) with the diagonal (dashed), for $w = 10$. The magenta arrows underneath show which way $a$ moves: right where the curve lies above the diagonal, left where it lies below. Filled dots are stable, open dots unstable. (a) Low input: one crossing, near rest. (b) Middle input: three crossings, two of them stable. (c) High input: one crossing, near saturation.

**Which way does the state move?** Equation (2.1) says $\dot a > 0$ exactly when $f(wa+I) > a$, that is, when the black curve is *above* the diagonal. So the arrows point right wherever the curve is above the line and left wherever it is below. A fixed point where the curve crosses the diagonal **from above to below** (going left to right) has arrows pointing into it from both sides: it is stable. A crossing from below to above has arrows pointing away: it is unstable. Stable and unstable crossings must alternate, as they do in panel (b).

**The algebra agrees.** Linearise (2.1) at a fixed point $a^*$: a small displacement $\xi$ obeys $\tau\dot\xi = (-1 + w f'(wa^* + I))\,\xi$. The fixed point is stable when
$$
w\,f'(w a^* + I) < 1 ,
$$
that is, when **the gain curve is less steep than the diagonal** where they cross. This is the slope condition read off the picture.

:::key Why one population can be bistable
Three crossings need the S-shaped curve to cut the diagonal three times, so its steepest part must be steeper than the diagonal: $w\,\beta/4 > 1$, or $w > 4/\beta$. **Recurrent excitation must be strong enough to amplify a small increase in activity into a larger one.** With weak recurrence the curve is too flat, and only one crossing is possible for any input.
:::

## Turning the input knob: saddle-nodes

Changing $I$ slides the gain curve sideways (Figure 2.2): raising $I$ moves it left, because the same activity now produces more input. Watch the crossings:

- At low $I$ there is one crossing, near $a = 0$.
- As $I$ rises, the curve's upper bend touches the diagonal and **a new pair of crossings is born**, one stable and one unstable. Now there are three.
- As $I$ rises further, the lower bend lifts off the diagonal: the low stable state and the unstable state **merge and vanish**. One crossing is left, near $a = 1$.

Each birth or death of a pair is a **saddle-node bifurcation** (Strogatz §3.1), the generic way fixed points appear and disappear in a one-dimensional flow. At the bifurcation the curve is tangent to the diagonal, $w f'(wa^*+I) = 1$, the borderline of the stability condition.

::fig ch02_slide | Left: the gain curve sliding as $I$ goes from $-4$ to $4$ and back; fixed points (dots) appear and disappear in pairs. Right: the fixed points against $I$ (the S-curve of Figure 2.3), with the current input marked.

## The S-curve and hysteresis

Plot every fixed point against $I$ and you get an **S-shaped curve** (Figure 2.3). For this one equation it can be drawn exactly: solve $a = f(wa+I)$ for $I$,
$$
I(a) = \theta - \frac{1}{\beta}\ln\!\Big(\frac{1}{a} - 1\Big) - w a ,\qquad 0<a<1,
$$
and plot $a$ against $I(a)$. The middle branch is unstable, and the two turning points are the saddle-nodes. AUTO, run from Python with pyauto-xpp, finds them at $I = -1.81$ and $I = 1.79$ (orange squares).

::fig ch02_hysteresis | Steady states of one population ($w = 10$, $\beta = 1$, $\theta = 5$) against input $I$: stable branches solid, unstable branch dashed, saddle-node points from AUTO (orange). Magenta and teal: the same population simulated in XPPAUT while $I$ is ramped slowly up and then down. Going up, the activity stays low until the lower saddle-node disappears near $I \approx 1.8$, then jumps up. Coming down, it stays high until $I\approx -1.8$, then drops. The path depends on history: **hysteresis**.

Why does the ramp jump only *after* the saddle-node, not at it? Because a state takes time to leave the place where a saddle-node has just vanished: the flow there is still very slow (a “ghost” of the fixed point, Strogatz §4.3). The faster the ramp, the later the jump.

Hysteresis is the form of **memory** Wilson and Cowan emphasised. In the bistable range, the current state tells you about the past: a high state means the input was high at some point. Figure 2.4 shows the simplest use. A brief positive pulse kicks the population over the unstable middle state into the high state, where it stays after the pulse ends; a brief negative pulse switches it off again. This is the basic picture of **persistent activity** in working-memory models of prefrontal cortex.

::fig ch02_switch | A bistable population ($I = 0$) as a memory. A brief positive pulse (top) switches activity from the low state to the high state, which persists; a brief negative pulse switches it back.

## Two knobs: the cusp

Bistability needs strong enough recurrence. To see how strong, continue the two saddle-nodes in *two* parameters, $I$ and $w$ (AUTO does this directly, Figure 2.5). The two curves of folds meet at a **cusp** at $w = 4/\beta = 4$, $I = \theta - 2 = 3$. Above the cusp the population is bistable for a range of inputs; below it, never.

::fig ch02_cusp | The two saddle-node curves of (2.1) in the $(I, w)$ plane, computed by AUTO. Inside the wedge the population has two stable states; outside, one. The wedge closes at the cusp $w = 4/\beta$, exactly where the steepest slope of the gain curve equals the slope of the diagonal.

:::paper Where this appears in the review and in Wilson & Cowan (1972)
The review stresses that Wilson and Cowan “showed that their equations exhibit multistability and hysteresis, which could serve as a substrate for memory”, citing the old analogy between memory and ferromagnetic hysteresis (Cragg & Temperley 1955). Their Figures 4–9 are hysteresis loops for an excitatory–inhibitory pair, the two-population version of Figure 2.3, which we reproduce in Chapter 7. The one-population model (2.1) is also the “STM model” for a single unit (Grossberg, Chapter 11), and Griffith (1963) had already noted that a population with only excitation can be bistable (Chapter 10).
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- Fixed points of $\tau\dot a = -a + f(wa+I)$ are crossings of the gain curve with the diagonal. A crossing is stable when the curve is less steep than the diagonal there.
- Strong recurrence ($w > 4/\beta$) allows three crossings: two stable states separated by an unstable one.
- Changing the input slides the curve; pairs of fixed points appear and vanish in **saddle-node bifurcations**.
- The fixed points form an **S-curve** against $I$. A slow ramp traces a **hysteresis loop**, and a brief pulse can switch the population between states: **memory**.
- In the $(I, w)$ plane the bistable region is a wedge ending at a **cusp**.
:::
