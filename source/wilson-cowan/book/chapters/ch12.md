The Wilson–Cowan equations rest on **quasi-stationarity**: activity changes slowly compared with the time over which it is averaged (Chapter 4). Feldman and Cowan showed in 1975 that the equations are valid exactly in that regime. What happens outside it? This chapter follows Gerstner's answer, the **spike-response formalism**, which derives population dynamics from single-neuron dynamics, says *when* a rate model is accurate, and shows what it misses: fast transients and synchrony.

::deck

## The picture first: a population that remembers its spike times

Give 300 neurons, all at rest, a step of input. If they are nearly identical, they all reach threshold at about the same moment and fire **together**. They then reset together, recharge together and fire together again: the population activity is a train of sharp volleys, which only slowly smear out (Figure 12.1a, c). A rate model, $\dot r = -r + F(\text{input})$, sees none of this. It rises smoothly to the new steady rate.

If instead the neurons are heterogeneous, each fires at its own rate, the phases scatter after the first volley, and the population settles quickly to a steady **asynchronous** state that the rate model describes well (Figure 12.1b, d).

::fig ch12_transient | Population response to a step of input at $t = 10$ (dashed), simulated in XPPAUT with 300 uncoupled θ-neurons whose excitabilities follow a Lorentzian distribution of width $\Delta$. (a, c) Nearly identical neurons: synchronous volleys at the single-neuron period, with peaks over ten times the steady rate. (b, d) Heterogeneous neurons: one overshoot, then asynchronous firing. Dashed blue: a first-order rate model with the exact steady-state gain $F$ of the population (dotted line). It gets the final rate right in both cases but misses the volleys entirely. (The activity before $t \approx 3$ is the neurons settling from random initial phases.)

:::key Why a rate model cannot see volleys
A rate model has one variable per population, the rate. A volley needs more: *where in its cycle* each neuron is. After a step all neurons start their cycles together, so the population carries a memory of the step time, and that memory is only erased (by heterogeneity or noise) over several cycles. Averaging over a window longer than the volleys is exactly the coarse-graining of Chapter 4, and it throws this information away.
:::

## Gerstner's spike-response model

To keep track of spike timing, Gerstner wrote the membrane potential of neuron $i$ as a sum of two **kernels** (Figure 12.2):
$$
u_i(t) = \eta\big(t - \hat t_i\big) + \sum_j w_{ij}\sum_f \kappa\big(t - t_j^{f}\big) + I^{\rm ext}_i, \tag{12.1}
$$
where $\hat t_i$ is the neuron's own last spike, $\eta$ is the **refractory kernel** (the reset and recovery after a spike), and $\kappa$ is the **response kernel** (the postsynaptic potential caused by an input spike at time $t_j^f$). Different single-neuron models, from integrate-and-fire to reductions of Hodgkin–Huxley, correspond to different choices of $\eta$ and $\kappa$.

::fig ch12_srm | The spike-response model. (a) Refractory kernel $\eta$: after its own spike the potential is pulled down and recovers. (b) Response kernel $\kappa$: the potential change caused by one input spike. (c) The membrane potential (12.1) for a random train of input spikes (ticks at the bottom): responses add up, and each threshold crossing fires a spike and adds a fresh refractory dip.

**From neurons to a population.** Gerstner then applied mean field theory. Neurons in a pool share their connections and kernels; **noise** (an escape rate: the probability of firing grows smoothly with $u$) makes their spike trains differ. If synapses are weak, each spike train is a **renewal process**: the next spike depends only on the time since the last one. This gives a closed **integral equation** for the pool activity $A(t)$, in which the fraction of neurons whose last spike was at time $\hat t$ plays the role of Wilson and Cowan's sensitive fraction, but now with the full refractory history kept.

## When are rate models valid?

From the integral equation Gerstner derived the correction to quasi-stationarity and a condition for a rate (differential-equation) model to be accurate:
$$
\langle \Delta t_{\rm isi}\rangle\;\frac{\dot A(t)}{A(t)} \ll 1, \tag{12.2}
$$
with $\langle\Delta t_{\rm isi}\rangle$ the mean interspike interval. In words: **the activity must change little during one interspike interval.** In Figure 12.1(c) the activity changes by a factor of more than ten within one interval, so (12.2) fails badly; in the asynchronous state of (d) $\dot A \approx 0$ and it holds.

He also re-derived the Wilson–Cowan equations from the spike-response formalism by choosing the kernels appropriately, with the noise-induced firing probability playing the role of Wilson and Cowan's sigmoid.

:::paper In the review
“Gerstner showed that differential equation activity models, while excellent for modeling asynchronous firing, break down for fast transients and coherent oscillations. He derived a correction to the quasistationarity assumption, estimating the error of rate-based models.” Earlier, Gerstner and van Hemmen (1992) had shown that for stationary activity any single-neuron model reduces to a network of graded-response neurons, “but this is not true for coherent oscillations.”
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- Rate models assume **quasi-stationarity**: activity changes slowly compared with one interspike interval.
- After a sudden input, identical neurons fire in **synchronous volleys**, which no first-order rate model can represent; heterogeneity or noise dephases them into asynchronous firing, which rate models describe well.
- Gerstner's **spike-response model** writes the potential as refractory kernel + summed response kernels; with escape noise and renewal statistics it yields an integral equation for the population activity.
- Validity condition: $\langle\Delta t_{\rm isi}\rangle\,\dot A/A \ll 1$.
:::
