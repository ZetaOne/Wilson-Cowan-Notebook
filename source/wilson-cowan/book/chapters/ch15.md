Chapter 14 showed that fluctuations shift the mean and that the moments form an endless hierarchy. How do we compute corrections to mean field theory *systematically*, to any order, and know when they are small? The review's answer is borrowed from quantum field theory: write the probability of every possible history of the network as a **path integral**, and expand it around its most probable path. Mean field theory is the first term; each further term is a correction in a small parameter such as $1/N$. This chapter builds the idea up from a one-dimensional integral, so the machinery has a picture behind every step.

::deck

## The picture: a mountain of probability

Suppose a quantity $x$ has probability density proportional to $e^{-S(x)/\epsilon}$, where $S$ has a minimum at $x = 0$ and $\epsilon$ is small. Then the density is a tall, narrow mountain centred on the minimum (Figure 15.1a). Three facts follow:

1. **Lowest order:** the most likely value, the minimum of $S$, is a good estimate of the mean. This is **mean field theory**: replace the random quantity by the critical point of $S$.
2. **Next order:** near its peak the mountain is a Gaussian with variance $\epsilon/S''(0)$. This is the **linear noise approximation** of Chapter 14.
3. **Corrections:** the mountain is not exactly symmetric. A cubic term in $S$ tilts it, so the mean slides off the peak by an amount proportional to $\epsilon$: $\langle x\rangle \approx -\epsilon\,S'''(0)/\big(2S''(0)^2\big)$.

This expansion of an integral around its peak is **Laplace's method**. In physics it is the semiclassical expansion, with $\epsilon$ playing the role of Planck's constant.

::fig ch15_laplace | Laplace's method. (a) $e^{-S(x)/\epsilon}$ for $S = x^2/2 - 0.2x^3 + x^4/4$ at three values of $\epsilon$: as $\epsilon$ shrinks the density concentrates at the minimum and approaches a Gaussian (dashed). (b) The mean's distance from the minimum, computed exactly by numerical integration (dots), against the first correction $0.6\,\epsilon$ (line): mean field theory is exact as $\epsilon \to 0$, and the first correction is proportional to $\epsilon$.

## From one variable to whole histories

For a network the random object is not a number but a **history**: the activity of every neuron at every time. The review (following Buice, Chow and collaborators) writes the probability of a history as follows.

1. The Klimontovich equation (13.3) and the synaptic equation define each history exactly, given the initial condition. So the probability of a history is a Dirac delta functional that enforces those equations.
2. A delta function can be written as a Fourier integral, $\delta(G) = \int \frac{\dd\tilde x}{2\pi}\,e^{i\tilde x G}$. Doing this at every time and place introduces an auxiliary **response field** $\tilde\eta(\theta, t)$ for each field $\eta(\theta, t)$ (the Martin–Siggia–Rose–Janssen–de Dominicis, MSRJD, construction).
3. Averaging over random initial conditions puts uncertainty into the otherwise deterministic system.

The result has exactly the form of the one-dimensional example, with a sum over histories in place of an integral over $x$:
$$
P[\eta, s] \;\propto\; \int \mathcal D\tilde\eta\,\mathcal D\tilde s\; e^{-N\,S[\eta, \tilde\eta, s, \tilde s]}. \tag{15.1}
$$
$S$ is the **action**, and the population size $N$ multiplies it. So $\epsilon = 1/N$: a large population is a sharply peaked mountain, and the expansion of Figure 15.1 applies. The most probable history (the critical point, $\delta S = 0$) satisfies the mean field equations (13.5). The Gaussian width gives the covariances, and the asymmetry gives corrections to the mean of order $1/N$.

:::idea Why Gaussian integrals do all the work
Every term in the expansion reduces to a Gaussian integral, and Gaussian integrals can be done exactly in any number of dimensions, even infinitely many. All moments of a Gaussian follow from its mean and covariance by **Wick's theorem**: for example $\langle x_1x_2x_3x_4\rangle = \Delta_{12}\Delta_{34} + \Delta_{13}\Delta_{24} + \Delta_{14}\Delta_{23}$ for zero mean, summing over all ways of pairing. For a dynamical system the covariance $\Delta$ is the **propagator**: the linear response at time $t$ to a kick at time $t'$, a Green's function.
:::

## Diagrams: trees and loops

The terms of the expansion grow quickly in number. They are organised by **Feynman diagrams** (Figure 15.2):

- **lines** are propagators (how a perturbation travels forward in time);
- **vertices** are the nonlinear (non-quadratic) terms of the action, where perturbations interact.

Mean field theory consists of **tree** diagrams with no closed paths: perturbations travel forward and combine, but fluctuations never feed back. Each order in $1/N$ adds one **loop**, in which a fluctuation is created, propagates, and returns to affect the mean. That is why the semiclassical expansion is also called the **loop expansion**.

::fig ch15_diagrams | The building blocks of the loop expansion. Left: a propagator. Middle: a tree diagram, the kind that makes up mean field theory. Right: a one-loop diagram: a fluctuation closes on itself and shifts the mean by an amount of order $1/N$.

## The effective action and generalised activity equations

Rather than expanding the mean field solution term by term, one can derive **closed equations of motion** for the moments themselves. A Legendre transformation of the cumulant generating functional gives the **effective action** $\Gamma[a]$, whose stationary point is the *true* mean including all fluctuation corrections. Including an auxiliary field for second moments gives the two-particle-irreducible effective action $\Gamma[a, C]$ for the mean and the covariance together. To first order in $1/N$ (Buice and Chow, 2013) its equations of motion are a set of **generalised activity equations**: Wilson–Cowan-like equations for the mean activity coupled to equations for the correlations.

For the two-state population of Chapter 14 they are the moment equations (14.5), truncated at the covariance:
$$
\dot{\bar a} = F(\bar a) + \tfrac12F''(\bar a)\,C, \qquad \dot C = 2F'(\bar a)\,C + \frac{G(\bar a)}{N}. \tag{15.2}
$$
Figure 15.3 tests them. Started with all neurons quiescent, a population of 40 neurons switches on to a lower level than mean field theory predicts: fluctuations, acting through the curvature of $F$, feed back on the mean. Equations (15.2), integrated in XPPAUT, track the average of 3000 exact simulations (final values $0.428$ against $0.429$, while mean field theory gives $0.456$). At steady state the shift of the mean approaches the one-loop value $N(\langle a\rangle - a^*) \to F''\sigma^2N/(2|F'|) = -0.78$ as $N$ grows. For populations of only 10–40 neurons it is larger: the higher loops (powers $1/N^2, \dots$) still matter.

::fig ch15_oneloop | Beyond mean field for the stochastic population of Chapter 14. (a) Mean activity after switching on, for $N = 40$: average of 3000 exact (Gillespie) simulations (magenta), mean field theory (dashed blue) and the one-loop generalised activity equations (15.2) (black, XPPAUT). (b) The steady-state shift of the mean from the mean field value, multiplied by $N$: exact values from the stationary distribution of the master equation (blue line), long simulations (dots with standard errors), and the one-loop prediction (dashed), which the exact values approach as $N$ grows.

:::paper In the review
“Mean field theory is the lowest order of the semiclassical expansion. Corrections at each successive power of $\epsilon$ require the computation of integrals that grow in complexity and number quickly, but the procedure can be systematized by the use of Feynman diagrams … The small parameter $\epsilon$ quantifies a deviation from mean field theory and could be the inverse of the number of neurons in a network of spiking neurons or a more abstract quantity such as a measure of the inverse distance of a network parameter to a bifurcation or critical point.” The generalised activity equations “represent a set of generalized activity equations for the mean activity and correlations between pairs of neurons. The procedure could be extended to any set of cumulants.”
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- Laplace's method: an integral of $e^{-S/\epsilon}$ is dominated by the minimum of $S$. Mean field theory is the lowest order; Gaussian fluctuations come next; asymmetry shifts the mean by $O(\epsilon)$.
- The MSRJD construction turns the network's equations into a **path integral** over histories with action $N\,S$, so $\epsilon = 1/N$.
- Terms are organised by **Feynman diagrams**: trees are mean field theory, each loop adds a power of $1/N$.
- The **effective action** gives closed **generalised activity equations** for means and correlations, which correct mean field theory for finite networks.
:::
