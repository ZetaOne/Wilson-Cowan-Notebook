Wilson and Cowan did not invent population models from nothing. The review devotes a section to what came before: thirty years in which the key ingredients (threshold units, statistical rather than wired-up descriptions, coarse-graining in space, the role of inhibition, a gain function) appeared one by one. This chapter follows that history, and for each step shows the picture that made the next step necessary.

## 1943: McCulloch and Pitts, neurons as logic

Neurons fire all-or-none when their input passes a threshold. McCulloch and Pitts took this literally: a neuron is a **binary threshold unit** that outputs 1 when the weighted sum of its binary inputs reaches a threshold $h$,
$$
y = \begin{cases}1 & \text{if } \sum_i w_i x_i \geq h,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
$$
With excitatory ($w > 0$) and inhibitory ($w < 0$) inputs such units implement AND, OR and NOT (Figure 10.2), and hence, networked together, **any logical computation**. This launched the field of neural networks in computer science, artificial intelligence and neuroscience.

::fig ch10_mcp | McCulloch–Pitts units as logic gates. Each unit fires when the weighted sum of its inputs reaches its threshold $h$. Two excitatory inputs with $h = 2$ give AND, with $h = 1$ OR; one inhibitory input with $h = 0$ gives NOT.

## 1948: Shimbel and Rapoport, statistics instead of wiring

Shimbel and Rapoport objected. If brains were logic circuits, countless different circuits would compute the same thing, and losing a few neurons would break them; real brains are robust. Genes cannot specify every synapse either, only *statistical* features of connectivity. So the goal should be to find the **statistical laws** governing the activity of a region, not to wire up a circuit for each task.

They wrote a recursive equation for the **fraction of neurons firing** at each location, built from probabilities: the probability that a neuron has a given threshold and synapse type, and that its input comes from a given place. Two ideas from this work survive in every activity equation: the next state depends only on the current one (a Markov property), and the firing probability depends only on the *mean* input, not on higher statistics. The second is the mean field assumption of Chapter 1.

## 1956: Beurle, the first neural field

Beurle replaced individual synapses by **densities**: anatomists had begun to measure how the number of connections falls off with distance (Sholl), giving a connection function $w(x) = b\,e^{-|x|/a}$. Activity became a continuous field $A(x, t)$, the proportion of cells active per unit time, with input
$$
s(x, t) = \int\!\!\int w(x - x')\,A(x', t')\,\alpha(t - t')\,\dd x'\,\dd t',
$$
exactly the form of the neural fields of Chapter 8. Beurle's cells were all **excitatory**, with a uniform threshold and a refractory period. He found plane, spherical and circular waves and vortices.

**The problem is visible on a line.** With only excitation, a cell that needs several coincident inputs has a gain that *accelerates* from zero. Draw $\dot A = -A + (1 - A)\,g(A)$ (Figure 10.3a): the only intermediate steady state sits where the curve crosses zero going *up*, so it is unstable. A slight deviation either way and activity **dies** or **saturates** (limited only by refractoriness). Beurle concluded, wrongly as it turned out, that there could be no stable ongoing random activity.

::fig ch10_beurle | One-population pictures of the two pre-1972 theories, simulated in XPPAUT. (a, c) Beurle: excitation only, with a gain that accelerates from zero. The intermediate state (open circle) is unstable: activity from any start dies out or saturates. (b, d) Griffith: a sigmoidal gain with a small spontaneous output creates a stable **low** state (filled circle near zero), so weak activity settles there instead of vanishing.

## 1963–1965: Griffith, inhibition and the gain function

Griffith first argued that **inhibition** is needed to stabilise brain activity. He then took a different route to a field theory: he asked what equation a field of synaptic excitation $s(x, t)$ must obey if it is driven by activity $A = f(s)$ and respects causality and translational and rotational symmetry. Expanding to second order, he arrived at a reaction–diffusion equation, which in its simplest form reads
$$
\partial_t s = \nabla^2 s - \mu s + f(s), \tag{10.1}
$$
and, ignoring space, $\dot s = -\mu s + f(s)$: the STM equation of Chapter 4 for one population.

Griffith showed that Beurle's theory was equivalent to his with a suitable $f$, and that the **shape of the gain function decides** whether stable spontaneous activity exists (Figure 10.3b). With a bistable $f$, (10.1) carries travelling fronts of excitation (Figure 10.4), the reaction–diffusion analogue of the fronts of Chapter 8.

::fig ch10_griffith | Griffith's reaction–diffusion field (10.1) with a bistable $f$, simulated in XPPAUT on 200 points. (a) Space–time plot: the excited state invades the resting one at constant speed. (b) Snapshots: the front keeps its shape as it moves.

:::idea Why the front moves, and when it stalls
For (10.1) a front between the states $s_-$ and $s_+$ moves towards the state with the *smaller* “potential”, $V(s) = \int (\mu s - f(s))\,\dd s$. If $\int_{s_-}^{s_+}(f(s) - \mu s)\,\dd s > 0$ the excited state wins and invades; if the integral is zero the front stalls. (In making Figure 10.4, the first choice of $f$ happened to give exactly zero, and the front sat still.)
:::

## What was still missing

By the mid-1960s, the review notes, “most of the concepts that would be incorporated into the Wilson–Cowan equations had been proposed, although no one had put them together into a cohesive whole.” Griffith's theory had a gain function and a stable spontaneous state, but no separate excitatory and inhibitory populations. Wilson and Cowan's contribution (Chapter 4) was to derive a coarse-grained theory, from threshold neurons with a refractory period, in which **excitation and inhibition are separate variables**.

:::paper In the review
The section “Before Wilson and Cowan” makes the point that the statistical branch began as a *dissent* from McCulloch and Pitts, and that the same dissent (Ashby, von Neumann) shaped Rosenblatt's perceptron: “the argument against the logical circuit approach instigated advances in machine learning as well.”
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- **McCulloch–Pitts (1943):** binary threshold units compute logic.
- **Shimbel–Rapoport (1948):** describe statistics of activity, not wiring; firing probability depends only on the mean input (mean field).
- **Beurle (1956):** coarse-grain in space, the first neural field; with excitation only, activity dies or saturates.
- **Griffith (1963–65):** inhibition stabilises; a reaction–diffusion field; the gain function decides whether stable spontaneous activity exists.
- **Wilson–Cowan (1972):** separate E and I populations, derived from threshold neurons with refractoriness.
:::
