Every activity equation has an S-shaped gain function $f$ at its heart, and everything in Chapter 2 depended on its shape: where it is steep, where it saturates, how many times it can cross a line. Where does it come from? Wilson and Cowan gave a beautifully simple answer: **the sigmoid is the cumulative distribution of thresholds across the population.** This chapter builds that answer geometrically, checks it on a simulated population, and then explains the second ingredient of their equations, the **refractory factor** $(1 - r\,a)$.

::deck

## A population of threshold cells

Imagine a population where each cell fires if its input exceeds its own threshold $\theta$. Give every cell the same input $x$. Which cells fire? Exactly those with $\theta < x$. So the **fraction of cells that fire** is the fraction whose threshold lies below $x$:
$$
f(x) = \int_0^{x} p(\theta)\,\dd\theta, \tag{3.1}
$$
where $p(\theta)$ is the probability density of thresholds across the population. This is Wilson and Cowan's equation for the response function.

**Geometrically:** draw the threshold density; the gain at input $x$ is **the area under the density to the left of $x$** (Figure 3.1). As $x$ sweeps from left to right the shaded area grows. Slowly at first, where few cells have thresholds; quickly near the middle, where most do; and slowly again at the end, when almost everyone is already firing. That is an S.

::fig ch03_area | The gain function as an area. Top: the density of thresholds across the population; the shaded area is the fraction of cells whose threshold is below the input $x$. Bottom: that area against $x$ traces the sigmoid $f(x)$. The animation sweeps $x$ across.

Two facts follow at once from the picture:

- **The slope of $f$ is the density itself:** $f'(x) = p(x)$ (the fundamental theorem of calculus). The sigmoid is steepest where thresholds are most common.
- **Each peak of the density gives an inflection point of $f$.** A unimodal density gives one inflection, a single S. A population made of two groups with different typical thresholds gives a bimodal density and an f with two inflections, a double S (Figure 3.2). Wilson and Cowan pointed this out explicitly: “there will be one inflection point for each mode of the distribution.”

::fig ch03_thresholds | (a, c) A unimodal threshold density gives a single sigmoid. (b, d) A bimodal density, two groups of cells, gives a response with two inflection points (orange squares), one at each peak. In each case the dot marks the input $x = 4.2$, where the bottom curve's height equals the shaded area above.

:::note The other derivation: spread in synapses, not thresholds
Wilson and Cowan note (and the review's footnote repeats) that the same S arises if all cells share one threshold $\theta$ but differ in how many afferent synapses they have. With input $x$ per synapse, a cell with $w$ synapses fires if $w x > \theta$, so the fraction firing is $f(x) = \int_{\theta/x}^\infty p(w)\,\dd w$, again an increasing, saturating function of $x$. Any source of heterogeneity smooths the step.
:::

## A practical sigmoid

For calculations Wilson and Cowan replaced the integral by the logistic function
$$
\mathcal S(x) = \frac{1}{1 + \e^{-a(x-\theta)}} - \frac{1}{1 + \e^{a\theta}}, \tag{3.2}
$$
where $\theta$ is the threshold, $a$ the steepness, and the second term shifts the curve so that $\mathcal S(0) = 0$: no input, no activity. Its maximum value is $k = 1 - 1/(1 + \e^{a\theta})$, slightly less than 1. The logistic is the cumulative distribution of a density that looks almost exactly Gaussian, so it is an excellent stand-in for (3.1).

## Checking it on a simulated population

Is the gain function of a real population, made of neurons that fire *at rates* rather than just “firing or not”, really an average over cells? Figure 3.3 tests it. We simulate 150 uncoupled θ-neurons in XPPAUT. Neuron $i$ has its threshold shifted by $\theta_i$, drawn from a Gaussian with standard deviation 0.3, and all receive the same input $x$. Each neuron's rate is $\sqrt{x - \theta_i}/\pi$ above its threshold and zero below (Chapter 1).

::fig ch03_gain | (a) The rate of a population of 150 heterogeneous θ-neurons against a common input, measured in XPPAUT (dots), and the average of the single-neuron f–I curves (line): they agree. (b) The fraction of neurons above threshold, which is Wilson and Cowan's “fraction active” (3.1), compared with the Gaussian cumulative distribution.

Panel (a) is the population gain in the sense of Chapter 1: an **average of single-cell f–I curves**. This is the interpretation Feldman and Cowan (1975) made precise, and the review highlights it: “the activation function in the Wilson–Cowan equations can be regarded as the average of single-neuron activation functions.” Panel (b) is Wilson and Cowan's original “fraction that fires”. The two differ in detail, since a rate population keeps rising after every cell has crossed threshold, but both are smooth, monotone and saturating near threshold. That shape is what the dynamics in the rest of the book depends on.

## Refractoriness: who is able to fire?

A neuron that has just fired is briefly **refractory**: it cannot fire again for a time $r$, however strong its input. So at any moment only part of the population is **sensitive**, able to respond. Wilson and Cowan counted it. If $E(t)$ is the fraction of cells that become active per unit time, then the fraction that fired in the last $r$ time units is $\int_{t-r}^{t}E(t')\,\dd t'$, and the sensitive fraction is
$$
1 - \int_{t-r}^{t} E(t')\,\dd t'.
$$
If being sensitive and receiving enough input are independent (a mean field assumption), the fraction becoming active a short time $\tau$ later is the product:
$$
E(t+\tau) = \Big[1 - \int_{t-r}^{t}E(t')\,\dd t'\Big]\;\mathcal S\big(x(t)\big). \tag{3.3}
$$
This is their equation 9, an integral equation with memory over the past $r$ time units.

**The coarse-graining step.** Wilson and Cowan then averaged over a time window comparable to $r$, replacing $\int_{t-r}^t E\,\dd t'$ by $rE(t)$, and expanded $E(t+\tau) \approx E(t) + \tau\dot E$. The result is the familiar ODE
$$
\tau\,\dot E = -E + (1 - rE)\,\mathcal S(x), \tag{3.4}
$$
with the **refractory factor** $(1 - rE)$. Geometrically it is a ceiling: as $E$ approaches $1/r$, the factor shrinks to zero and no more cells can be recruited.

How much is lost by coarse-graining? Figure 3.4 reproduces Wilson and Cowan's own Figure 3. After a step of input, the integral equation (3.3) overshoots and rings: a burst of activity makes many cells refractory at once, activity drops, they recover together, and so on. The ODE (3.4) climbs smoothly to **the same steady state** $E^* = \mathcal S/(1 + r\mathcal S)$. As they wrote: “the solution to equation 10 approaches the same asymptotic value monotonically.” The coarse-grained equation gets the destination right and smooths over the journey, an early sign of the limits of rate equations that Chapter 12 explores.

::fig ch03_refractory | Wilson and Cowan's Figure 3, recomputed. Grey: the integral equation (3.3) with refractory period $r = 3$ (iterated as a discrete recursion). Magenta: the coarse-grained ODE (3.4), integrated with XPPAUT. Both settle at $\mathcal S/(1 + r\mathcal S)$ (dotted), but only the integral equation shows the damped ringing caused by cells recovering from refractoriness in step.

:::paper The review on this derivation
“They assumed that neurons had a sensitive phase where input exceeding a threshold would cause them to fire and a refractory period in which they would not fire regardless of input … If this and the fraction of sensitive neurons are independent, then the updated fraction of active cells would be $[1 - \int_{t-r}^t A_i(t')\,\dd t']\,f_i(z_i)\,\delta t$.” The review also flags the weak point: “the input to a cell and the sensitive fraction could be correlated, which would violate mean field theory since both involve $A_i$.” Wilson and Cowan argued that this correlation is negligible in highly interconnected networks with variable thresholds. Part IV is about what happens when it is not.
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- The gain function is the **cumulative distribution of thresholds**: $f(x)$ = fraction of cells with threshold below $x$, the area under the threshold density.
- Its slope is the threshold density; each peak of the density gives an inflection point.
- A population of rate neurons has gain equal to the **average of single-cell f–I curves** (Feldman & Cowan), also sigmoid near threshold.
- Refractoriness limits the **sensitive fraction**, giving the factor $(1 - rE)$. Time coarse-graining turns the integral equation into an ODE with the same steady states but smoother dynamics.
:::

:::try Exercises
1. If thresholds are uniformly distributed on $[\theta_1, \theta_2]$, what is $f(x)$? *(Zero below $\theta_1$, a straight ramp between, one above: a piecewise-linear “sigmoid”.)*
2. Show that the steady state of (3.4) is $E^* = \mathcal S/(1 + r\mathcal S)$, and that $E^* < 1/r$ for every input.
3. Why does the integral equation (3.3) ring after a step while the ODE does not? *(The step makes a cohort of cells fire together; they become sensitive again together, after a time $r$, producing a second, smaller cohort, and so on. Averaging over a window of length $r$ erases the cohorts.)*
:::
