While Wilson and Cowan were deriving population equations from threshold neurons, a parallel line of work arrived at the same equations from a different question: how can a network *learn and remember*? This chapter follows that line, from Grossberg's short-term-memory (STM) models to the theorem of Cohen and Grossberg and to Hopfield's network, and draws the geometric picture that unites them: **flow downhill on an energy landscape**.

::deck

## Grossberg's STM equations

The artificial-intelligence branch that followed McCulloch and Pitts first studied binary units. Hartline and Ratliff (1958) instead modelled the *continuous* firing rates of the Limulus retina, and in the 1960s Rosenblatt's perceptron and Widrow's Adaline showed that adaptive networks could recognise patterns. Grossberg wanted to understand how behaviour can adapt stably, in real time, to a changing world. He wrote the **additive STM equation**
$$
\tau\dot s_i = -s_i + \sum_j w_{ij}\,f_j(s_j) + I_i, \tag{11.1}
$$
which is the STM form of Chapter 4: the inputs of a Wilson–Cowan network obey it. He then gave the weights their own slow dynamics (long-term memory), and derived a **shunting** variant modelled on the Hodgkin–Huxley membrane, in which excitation drives an activity towards an upper bound and inhibition towards a lower one:
$$
\dot x_i = -A x_i + (B - x_i)\,[\text{excitation}]_i - (x_i + C)\,[\text{inhibition}]_i . \tag{11.2}
$$
When the activities are far from their bounds, (11.2) reduces to (11.1).

## The geometric idea: a landscape that the state rolls down

Picture a ball rolling on a hilly surface with friction. Whatever its start, it ends at the bottom of some valley; it can never circle forever, because each loop would have to return to the same height while friction keeps removing energy. A function that only decreases along trajectories is a **Lyapunov function** (Strogatz §7.2). If a network has one, every trajectory ends at a steady state, and the steady states are the bottoms of the valleys. **Memories become valleys.**

:::thm Cohen–Grossberg (1983), in Hopfield's form (1984)
If the weights are **symmetric**, $w_{ij} = w_{ji}$, and each $f_i$ is increasing, then
$$
L(\vec u) = -\tfrac12\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}\,a_i a_j - \sum_i I_i a_i + \sum_i \int_0^{a_i} f_i^{-1}(x)\,\dd x, \qquad a_i = f_i(u_i),
$$
never increases along solutions of (11.1).
:::

**Why.** Differentiate $L$ with respect to one activity $a_i$. The double sum contributes $-\sum_j w_{ij}a_j$; this is where symmetry is used, because $a_i$ appears in both $w_{ij}a_ia_j$ and $w_{ji}a_ja_i$, and the two halves combine only when $w_{ij} = w_{ji}$. The integral contributes $f_i^{-1}(a_i) = u_i$. So
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_i} = -\sum_j w_{ij}a_j - I_i + u_i = -\tau\dot u_i .
$$
By the chain rule, with $\dot a_i = f_i'(u_i)\,\dot u_i$,
$$
\frac{\dd L}{\dd t} = \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_i}\,\dot a_i = -\tau\sum_i f_i'(u_i)\,\dot u_i^{\,2} \;\leq\; 0,
$$
because $f_i' > 0$ and squares are non-negative. Equality holds only where every $\dot u_i = 0$, at steady states.

## Asymmetric weights can cycle

The Wilson–Cowan E–I pair is **not** symmetric: $E$ excites $I$ ($+c_3$) while $I$ inhibits $E$ ($-c_2$). The two halves of the derivative no longer combine and the theorem fails. Geometrically, there is no landscape: the flow can circulate, and Chapter 6's limit cycle is exactly such a circulation (Figure 11.2).

::fig ch11_asym | The oscillating E–I network of Chapter 6, written in STM form. (a) Its limit cycle in the plane of inputs. (b) The energy formula built from the symmetrised weights is not conserved or decreased: it rises and falls every cycle. Asymmetric excitation–inhibition loops are what allow rhythms.

:::key Symmetric versus asymmetric
- **Symmetric weights:** a landscape; every trajectory settles; good for **memory** (Grossberg, Cohen–Grossberg, Hopfield).
- **Asymmetric weights** (as with separate E and I populations): no landscape in general; oscillations and more complex dynamics; good for **rhythms** (Wilson–Cowan, Chapter 6).
:::

## Hopfield's network: memories as valleys

In 1982 Hopfield, using the analogy with spin glasses in statistical physics, showed that a network of binary neurons with symmetric connections has a non-increasing energy, and in 1984 he extended this to graded-response neurons, the setting of the theorem above. To **store** patterns $\vec\xi^{\,\mu}$ (vectors of $\pm1$), use Hebb's rule:
$$
w_{ij} = \frac1N\sum_\mu \xi^\mu_i\,\xi^\mu_j .
$$
The weights are symmetric, so the energy theorem applies, and each stored pattern sits at the bottom of a valley. Start the network from a corrupted version of a pattern and it rolls into the nearest valley: **content-addressable memory** (Figure 11.3).

Starting from pattern 1 with 18 of its 64 pixels flipped, the state completes the pattern within a few time units. (g) Overlaps $m_\mu = \frac1N\sum_i \xi^\mu_i\,g_i$ with each stored pattern. (h) The energy decreases monotonically to its value at the valley bottom.

:::paper In the review
The review is pointed about priority: Hopfield's contributions “attracted a lot of interest from the physics community … but it is rarely acknowledged that his discovery was a special case of the general Cohen–Grossberg theorem and that there was much work in this area that preceded him.” It also notes that the analogy between neural networks and spin glasses had been pointed out earlier by Little and Shaw (1974, 1978).
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- Grossberg's additive STM model is the input form of the Wilson–Cowan equations; the shunting model adds bounds.
- With **symmetric** weights there is an energy (Cohen–Grossberg 1983; Hopfield 1984) that decreases along every trajectory: every orbit settles at a steady state.
- Hebbian weights put stored patterns at the bottoms of valleys: content-addressable memory.
- **Asymmetric** E–I coupling breaks the energy picture and allows oscillations.
:::
