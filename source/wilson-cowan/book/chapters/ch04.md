Cortex is not one population but at least two: **excitatory** cells (pyramidal neurons, about 80% of cortical neurons), which make other cells more likely to fire, and **inhibitory** cells (interneurons), which make them less likely. Wilson and Cowan's 1972 paper was the first to write down a coarse-grained description that keeps the two apart. This chapter derives their equations step by step, from the sensitive-fraction argument of Chapter 3, and then shows a useful fact the review emphasises: the same dynamics can be written for the **activities** or for the **inputs**.

::deck

## The circuit

Two populations, $E$ (excitatory) and $I$ (inhibitory), each receiving input from itself, from the other, and from outside (Figure 4.1):

- $c_1$: excitatory cells excite each other (recurrent excitation);
- $c_2$: inhibitory cells inhibit excitatory cells;
- $c_3$: excitatory cells excite inhibitory cells;
- $c_4$: inhibitory cells inhibit each other;
- $P$ and $Q$: external input to $E$ and to $I$.

::fig ch04_circuit | The Wilson–Cowan circuit. Two populations with four connection strengths and two external inputs. Excitatory connections ($c_1, c_3$) are drawn as arrows, inhibitory ones ($c_2, c_4$) as bars.

Physiology motivated the split. Hubel and Wiesel and Mountcastle had found columns of cells with similar responses, Szentágothai had described local circuits of excitatory and inhibitory cells, and **Dale's principle** says a neuron is either excitatory or inhibitory at all its synapses. So $E$ and $I$ are not just signs on a weight matrix but two kinds of cells.

## Step 1: who is sensitive, who gets enough input

Repeat the argument of Chapter 3 for each population. Let $E(t)$ and $I(t)$ be the proportions of excitatory and inhibitory cells **becoming active** per unit time.

- **Sensitive fraction.** Cells that fired within the last refractory period $r$ cannot fire, so the sensitive fraction of $E$ cells is $1 - \int_{t-r}^{t} E(t')\,\dd t'$.
- **Input.** A sensitive cell fires if its input exceeds its threshold. Its average input sums recent activity, weighted by the connection strengths and by a decaying response function $\alpha(t)$ (a postsynaptic potential lingers after the spike):
$$
x_E(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t}\alpha(t - t')\,\big[c_1 E(t') - c_2 I(t') + P(t')\big]\,\dd t'.
$$
- **Fraction firing** = (sensitive fraction) × (fraction of sensitive cells whose threshold is exceeded) = $\big[1 - \int_{t-r}^{t}E\big]\;\mathcal S_E(x_E)$, with $\mathcal S_E$ the sigmoid of Chapter 3.

So, after a short delay $\tau$,
$$
E(t+\tau) = \Big[1 - \int_{t-r}^{t}E(t')\,\dd t'\Big]\;\mathcal S_E\!\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{t}\alpha(t-t')\,[c_1E - c_2I + P](t')\,\dd t'\Big), \tag{4.1}
$$
and similarly for $I$ with $c_3, c_4, Q$ and $\mathcal S_I$. These are the review's equations (8)–(9). They are exact under the mean field assumption, but they are integral equations with memory, hard to analyse.

## Step 2: coarse-grain in time

Wilson and Cowan replaced the instantaneous proportions by their averages over a short window, and assumed the response function $\alpha$ is roughly constant for about one refractory period and then decays quickly. Then the two integrals simplify:
$$
\int_{t-r}^{t}E(t')\,\dd t' \;\to\; r\,E(t), \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{t}\alpha(t-t')\,E(t')\,\dd t' \;\to\; k\,E(t),
$$
with constants $r$ and $k$. Geometrically, each integral is the area under the recent history of $E$; if $E$ changes little over the window, that area is just height × width. Expanding $E(t + \tau) \approx E(t) + \tau\dot E(t)$ and absorbing $k$ into the weights gives

:::thm The Wilson–Cowan equations (1972)
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_E\,\dot E &= -E + (k_E - r_E E)\,\mathcal S_E\big(c_1E - c_2I + P\big),\\
\tau_I\,\dot I &= -I + (k_I - r_I I)\,\mathcal S_I\big(c_3E - c_4I + Q\big),
\end{aligned} \tag{4.2}
$$
with $\mathcal S(x) = 1/(1+\e^{-a(x-\theta)}) - 1/(1+\e^{a\theta})$ (separate $a_E, \theta_E$ and $a_I, \theta_I$) and $k = \max\mathcal S$.
:::

Read (4.2) exactly as in Chapter 1: **each activity relaxes towards (sensitive fraction) × (gain of its current input)**. The minus signs on $c_2$ and $c_4$ make inhibition subtract from the input.

:::warn What the coarse-graining assumes
Replacing an integral over the last $r$ time units by $r$ times the current value is accurate only if $E$ changes slowly on the time scale $r$, which is called **quasi-stationarity**. Feldman and Cowan (1975) showed that the Wilson–Cowan equations are valid exactly in this regime, and Gerstner later quantified the error (Chapter 12). Fast synchronous events, such as the ringing in Figure 3.4, are smoothed away.
:::

The review writes (4.2) compactly for many populations $i$:
$$
\tau\dot a_i = -a_i + (1 - r a_i)\,f_i\Big(\sum_j w_{ij}a_j + I_i(t)\Big), \tag{4.3}
$$
with $w_{ij}$ positive for excitatory and negative for inhibitory sources. This is the form we use throughout. In most modern work $r = 0$ (the refractory factor is dropped), which changes the quantitative behaviour but rarely the qualitative picture.

## Two ways to write the same dynamics: activities or inputs

There is a second common form of activity equation, which Grossberg called the additive **short-term-memory (STM)** model:
$$
\tau\dot s_i = -s_i + \sum_j w_{ij}\,f_j(s_j) + I_i . \tag{4.4}
$$
Here the variable $s_i$ is the population's *input* (synaptic drive, or a voltage-like quantity), not its activity. The review points out that for constant input, and with $r = 0$, (4.3) and (4.4) are the same system. The argument is short. Define the input of population $i$ in the WC model as
$$
s_i := \sum_j w_{ij}a_j + I_i .
$$
Differentiate (with $\tau = 1$ and $I_i$ constant), then use (4.3) for each $\dot a_j$:
$$
\dot s_i = \sum_j w_{ij}\,\dot a_j = \sum_j w_{ij}\big[-a_j + f_j(s_j)\big] = -\underbrace{\sum_j w_{ij}a_j}_{=\,s_i - I_i} + \sum_j w_{ij} f_j(s_j) = -s_i + \sum_j w_{ij}f_j(s_j) + I_i .
$$
That is (4.4). So **the inputs of a Wilson–Cowan network obey the STM equations**. Conversely, at a steady state $a_i = f_i(s_i)$: the activity is the gain of the input, which is why $a$ can be read as a firing rate.

:::idea Which variable to use?
Activities $a$ are bounded between 0 and 1, which is convenient for plots and interpretation (a fraction of active cells). Inputs $s$ are unbounded and enter the gain function directly, which is convenient for linear stability analysis and for neural fields (Chapter 8), where Amari's equation is usually written for the input. Physically, $s$ is closer to a membrane potential or synaptic current, and $a = f(s)$ to a firing rate. The review: “if $a$ is neural activity then $s$ is best viewed as a synaptic input or drive.”
:::

:::paper Section “What are the Wilson–Cowan equations?”
The review lists the many names the same equations go by (Wilson–Cowan, Cohen–Grossberg, Amari, rate equations, graded-response equations, STM equations, neural field equations), adopts the neutral name “activity equations”, and reserves “Wilson–Cowan equations” for (4.3) and “STM model” for (4.4). It also notes the continuum version of 1973, in which the index $i$ becomes a position $x$ and the sum becomes an integral (Chapter 8).
:::

:::recap What to carry forward
- Wilson–Cowan keep **excitatory and inhibitory populations apart** (Dale's principle), with four connections $c_1, \dots, c_4$ and inputs $P, Q$.
- The derivation: sensitive fraction × fraction above threshold (an integral equation), then time coarse-graining under quasi-stationarity, giving two ODEs (4.2).
- Each activity relaxes towards (refractory factor) × (sigmoid of its input).
- With constant input and $r=0$, the **inputs** $s = \sum w a + I$ obey Grossberg's STM equations: the two forms are equivalent.
:::
